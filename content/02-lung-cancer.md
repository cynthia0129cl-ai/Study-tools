# Lung Cancer

Lung cancer is the **#1 cancer killer in the US** for both men and women. **More than 90% is caused by smoking.** It is often silent until a late stage and is most common after age 65.

## Why it matters and risk factors
- ACS 2022: lung cancer is the 2nd most common cancer in both men and women and the leading cause of cancer death (~130,180 deaths).
- Survival depends on stage at diagnosis. **Stage IA has 49% survival; stage IV has 1%.**
- Black men have higher incidence than White men. White women have higher incidence than Black women.
- *SLIDE vs BOOK:* one slide says "leading cause of cancer," but the book (and another slide) says 2nd most common cancer and #1 cause of cancer death.

**Risk factors:**
- **Smoking (90%):** risk rises with the number of cigarettes and the years smoked. The most preventable cause.
- Second-hand smoke
- Radon
- Occupational pollutants, asbestos (also coal, soot, tar, wood dust, pollution per Ch 13)
- **Screening:** yearly low-dose CT for high-risk patients (≥20 pack-years, current smoker or quit within 15 years).

:::patho Pathophysiology
Carcinogens such as cigarette smoke cause dysplasia of the lung epithelium. Repeated exposure leads to genetic mutations: oncogenes switch on, tumor suppressors switch off, cells evade apoptosis, and DNA repair fails. The result is uncontrolled growth of abnormal cells (carcinogenesis).
:::

## SCLC vs NSCLC ★ (know this difference)

| | Small cell ("oat cell") | Non–small cell |
|---|---|---|
| % of lung cancers | **15%** | **85%** |
| Cell types | Small (oat) cells | **3 types: adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, large cell carcinoma** |
| Behavior | Grows quickly and spreads to other organs | Slower |
| Prognosis | **Poor at any stage.** Only 5–10% survive 5 years. **Class: life expectancy 6 months–1 year** | **Good** 5-year survival if found at an early stage |
| Surgery? | Not the main treatment | **Surgery is used primarily for NSCLC when there is no metastasis** |

:::note Class vs book
Your notes say small cell life expectancy is **6 months–1 year**. The book says only 5–10% survive 5 years. These fit together, but if a question asks for a number, the class number is 6 months–1 year.
:::

:::ss Clinical manifestations ("recognizing cues") ★
Usually not evident early; show up as the disease advances.
- A **new cough that won't go away**, or a change in a chronic cough
- **Hemoptysis** (small vessels rupturing)
- Shortness of breath, wheezing, hoarseness
- Chest pain
- **Headache and bone pain**, which suggest metastasis (brain, bone)
- Weight loss
- Frequent pneumonia or bronchitis
- Common metastasis sites (Ch 13): **brain, bone, adrenal gland, the other lung**. Lung tumors can also cause paraneoplastic syndromes such as **SIADH and hypercalcemia**.
:::

:::dx Diagnosis, and why each test is done
- **Chest x-ray / CT of the chest** find the pulmonary lesion.
- **Sputum cytology** may identify the specific tumor cells.
- **Bronchoscopy** (scope through the mouth or nose) shows the trachea and bronchi and allows biopsy.
- **Mediastinoscopy** is a surgical incision in the chest to view the mediastinum and biopsy.
- **PET scan** looks at **metabolism, not structure**. It detects areas of high activity (rapidly dividing cancer cells).
- **Bone and abdominal scans** look for metastasis.
:::

:::tx Nonsurgical treatment
Depends on the type, size, location, and stage of the tumor.
- **Chemotherapy:** primary treatment for advanced cancer or a patient too ill for surgery. Can be neoadjuvant (shrink first) or adjuvant (kill leftover cells).
- **Radiation:** used when surgery isn't an option, before or after surgery, or for palliation of symptoms and pain.
- **Pain management follows the WHO ladder.** Step 1: nonopioid (acetaminophen, NSAIDs). Step 2: moderate opioid (codeine, hydrocodone, oxycodone) plus a nonopioid. Step 3: strong opioid (morphine, fentanyl) plus a nonopioid. **Give around the clock, not on demand**, to avoid pain peaks.
- **Palliative care should begin AT DIAGNOSIS.** It manages pain, dyspnea, and nausea and improves quality of life.
:::

:::tx Surgery (preferred if no metastasis)
- **Lobectomy:** removes an entire lobe, when the cancer is localized to one lobe.
- **Pneumonectomy:** removes the entire lung, when cancer is spread diffusely through it.
- **Wedge resection:** removes a small section of a lobe, for cancer in one small area.
:::

## Nursing care

| Assessments, and why | Actions, and why |
|---|---|
| **SpO₂:** low because of poor gas exchange. <90% = significant problem | Give O₂ to keep SpO₂ ≥90% |
| **Temperature:** a fever means infection | **Semi-Fowler's position** *→ why: allows full lung expansion and better oxygenation* |
| **Breath sounds:** wheezes suggest airway obstruction. Rhonchi mean secretions in the upper airways | **Small, frequent meals plus supplements** *→ why: a large meal pushes on the diaphragm; supplements add calories for the work of breathing* |
| **Cough:** irritation of the tracheobronchial tree. Hemoptysis means small vessels are rupturing | **Give meds:** pain and anti-anxiety *→ why: relaxation lets the lungs expand better*; bronchodilators *→ why: open the airway and decrease the work of breathing* |
| **Pain:** increases respiratory rate and lowers quality of life | Monitor temperature, cough, pain, and breath sounds |
| **Appetite and weight:** chemo side effects and the work of breathing both cause weight loss | |

**Nursing diagnoses:** ineffective gas exchange · anxiety (can't breathe) · activity intolerance (fatigue, dyspnea) · pain

:::watch Post-op: lobectomy / pneumonectomy
- **Vital signs:** hypotension or tachycardia may mean excessive blood or fluid loss.
- **Breath sounds:** diminished or absent sounds may mean atelectasis.
- **Suture line:** red, warm, with thick yellow drainage means infection.
- **Chest tube:** excess bloody drainage suggests a bleed. Cloudy drainage suggests infection.
- **Water-seal chamber:** persistent bubbling = an air leak in the system.
- Keep the system **CLOSED** to prevent air leaks.
- **NEVER CLAMP THE CHEST TUBE!** *→ why: air or fluid builds up in the pleural space, worsening the pneumothorax and possibly causing a TENSION pneumothorax.*
:::

:::nursing Teaching, and why
- **Breathing techniques / pursed-lip breathing** *→ why: clear sputum, reduce breathlessness, remove trapped air*
- **Pace activities** *→ why: conserves energy and lowers the work of breathing*
- **Smoking cessation** *→ why: can change the course of the disease*
- Small frequent meals · medication regimen (proper inhaler use)
- **Take pain meds around the clock** *→ why: keeps a steady level of control instead of peaks of pain*
- **Outcomes:** pain controlled, enough appetite and weight, adequate oxygenation. Refer for pulmonary rehab (respiratory therapists) and palliative care.
:::

## From your slides: asthma case (Clifford Davis)
A 39-year-old with asthma, a 15-year one-pack-a-day smoker. He is in the **tripod position** using pursed-lip breathing and can't finish a conversation. HR 150, RR 40, bilateral wheezes, **SpO₂ 88%**. Treatment: O₂ (up to 96%), prednisone (he missed his daily anti-inflammatory), albuterol nebulizers ×3, peak flow below baseline, then IM prednisone. Discharged on a prednisone taper with an updated asthma action plan.

- **Most important part of management:** disease management through **education**, using an **asthma action plan built on peak flow readings**.
- ⚠ **Wheezing goes away AND breath sounds are diminished** means he is not moving air (worsening obstruction or exhaustion). **Emergency:** respiratory failure, possible need for ventilation. A **rising PaCO₂** signals impending failure.
- **Priority when a partially obstructed airway is suspected: airway management.** A major part of his treatment program: smoking cessation.
- **Rescue med** = short-acting β₂ agonist (albuterol). **Controller** = inhaled corticosteroid (rinse the mouth to prevent oral candidiasis). **Never stop prednisone suddenly (taper).** Asthma meds are not stopped when asthma is controlled. **Peak flow: blow 3 times and record the highest reading.**
