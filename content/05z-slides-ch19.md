:::slides Slide 1 · Primary vs. secondary immunodeficiency
Learning outcomes in Chapter 19: There are 8 learning outcomes to assist you in understanding the pathophysiological process of the immune dysfunction and correlation of clinical manifestations in order to develop a comprehensive plan of care and teaching plan for patients with immune dysfunction.

Introduction: Immune deficiency disorder is the lack or absence of adequate immune response · Opposite of autoimmune disorder · Affected people are more susceptible to infections and have no fully functioning immune system. There are 2 immune deficiency disorders: Primary · Secondary Primary & Secondary deficiency: Primary immunodeficiency – occurs from genetic or developmental abnormality. Defects from T & B cells, natural killer cells, phagocytic cells or the complement system · Secondary immunodeficiencies occur after birth secondary to disease process (malnutrition, cancer, drugs/radiation therapy, loss of spleen, exposure to chemicals, infection or stress)

Table 19.1 – Warning signs of PID: Eight or more new infections within 1 year · Two or more serious sinus infections within 1 year · Two or more months on antibiotics · Two or more pneumonias within 1 year · Failure of an infant to gain weight · Recurrent deep skin or organ abscess · Persistent thrush in the mouth or elsewhere on skin after age 1 · Need for IV antibiotics to clear infections · A family history of immune deficiency
:::

:::slides Slide 2 · XLA: the disease
Epidemiology: XLA – an inherited or congenital immune disorder · The most common type of Primary Immune Deficiency · Accounts for 50% of PID cases · 1 in 250,000 male newborns · XLA manifests approx 6 months of age Pathophysiology: Deficiency of BTK (Bruton Tyrosine Kinase) – gene essential in development of B lymphocytes – present in the X chromosome · BTK protein alerts B cells to mature – unable to mature, no antibody response · Hallmark: No circulating B cells or plasma cells · Lab studies show markedly reduced immunoglobulins (IgG < 200 mg/dL) · Prone to severe bacterial infections

[Image: X-Linked Recessive Genetic Inheritance of Disease] "X-Linked Recessive, Carrier Mother." An unaffected father (XY) and a carrier mother (one altered X) have four possible children: unaffected son, unaffected daughter, carrier daughter (one altered X, no disease), and affected son (his only X is altered). Each son has a 50% chance of being affected.

Clinical Manifestations: Infections of the ears, lungs, skin, conjunctiva, and central nervous system · Bacterial infections such as pharyngitis, sinusitis, otitis media, bronchitis and pneumonia · Presence of recurrent bacterial infections of the respiratory tract in childhood is the first indicator Medical Management – Diagnosis: Detailed family history – to detect abnormal immune response · B- lymphocyte surface marker assays – identifies specific cells involved in immune response · Western blot test – determines if the BTK protein is being expressed · Genetic testing – women with XLA · Chest or sinus X-ray Medical Management – Treatment: IV immune globulin (IVIG) – provides passive immunity – 1980's – Immunoglobulin G (IgG) – Gammaglobulin – protects against infection from various diseases; given every 3–4 weeks or weekly thru subQ injections (serum IgG) · Prophylactic antibiotics – low dose Complications: Severe, life-threatening bacterial infection – pneumonia, empyema, meningitis, sepsis, cellulitis, septic arthritis – 60% in children · Chronic viral enterovirus · Enteroviral encephalitis · Neurological deterioration
:::

:::slides Slide 3 · Assess, act, teach, evaluate
Assessment and analysis: Related to infection of the respiratory tract, skin, conjunctiva, or ears · Common findings – fever, change in eating patterns, irritability, decreased activity, changes in behaviour, in children – inconsolable crying Nursing diagnosis: Risk for infection related to compromised host defenses secondary to B-cell immunodeficiency Assessments: Vital signs – T? RR? · Assess lung sounds – adventitious? · Inspect eyes and ears, skin and nails, rashes and lesions – assess the color of the skin & texture · Take note of current meds · Evaluate the infant or child's behavior and eating patterns Actions: Administer IV gamma globulin as ordered – assess any problems post infusion · Administer prophylactic antibiotic therapy as ordered · Anticipate prompt treatment with antibiotics when infection is present Teaching: Teach parents of at-risk children about the warning signs · Institute precautions to prevent infection · Protect the patient from direct contact with anyone with a contagious illness · Safe food handling and storage practices · Signs and symptoms of infection and when to seek medical attention · Good hand washing · Information regarding genetic disorders and community support groups Evaluating care outcomes: Infection prevention · When to contact healthcare provider · Start antibody replacement early – have good prognosis
:::

:::slides Slide 4 · CVID, IgA & IgG subclass deficiency
Other B-Cell Deficiencies: Common Variable Immune Deficiency (CVID) – low levels of circulating B cells, similar to XLA except that it appears in adolescents or young adults (15–35 y/o) · Selective Immunoglobulin A – 50% affected overcome by age 14; develop anaphylactic reactions when IgA is administered · Immunoglobulin G Subclass Deficiency – risk for sinusitis, otitis media, PNA – Tx prophylactic abx; IVIG Type of Antibodies – 5 types of antibodies & function: IgG – triggers autoimmune disease · IgA – found in mucosal tissues · IgM – helps B cell remember pathogen · IgE – responsible for allergic response · IgD – signals the release of front-line IgM to fight disease and infection. Kick starts the immune response
:::

:::slides Slide 5 · DiGeorge: the disease
Primary Immune Dysfunction: T-cell Deficiencies: CD4 helper cells · CD8 cytotoxic T cells · Cell- mediated immune response – protects against fungal, protozoan, viral, and intracellular bacterial infections · Control malignant cell proliferation Epidemiology: Autosomal-dominant genetic condition – presence of one altered gene · Arises from 22nd chromosome · Spontaneously present at birth · DiGeorge syndrome is a congenital thymic hypoplasia, present at birth; gestational diabetes is implicated

[Image: Autosomal-Dominant Genetic Inheritance of DiGeorge's Syndrome] An affected father and an unaffected mother have four possible children: affected son, unaffected daughter, unaffected son, affected daughter. Each child has a 50% chance; boys and girls are equally affected.

Pathophysiology: Disturbance of normal development of pharyngeal pouches – occurs during 6–10 weeks gestation · Effects depend on which pharyngeal pouch is affected – dysfunction of thymus and parathyroid – facial deformities (cleft palate) – heart anomalies Clinical Manifestations: Recurrent infections – yeast, fungal, protozoa; viral – measles, chicken pox, rubella, candida · Cardiac abnormality – heart murmur · Abnormal faces – low set ears, wide set eyes · Thymic aplasia – atrophy of the thymus · Cleft palate · Hypocalcemia – hypoparathyroidism – twitching, tingling in the lips, around the mouth Diagnosis: Genetic testing – finding of submicroscopic deletion of chromosome 22, detected by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) – molecular cytogenetic technique that allows the localization of specific DNA Treatment: Pharmacologic therapy – calcium supplements – prevents tetany & seizures · Treatment of infections · IVIG – provides ST passive immunity (3–4 weeks) · Prophylactic antibiotics · Bone marrow transplant in severe cases Surgical Management: Thymus tissue transplant (3–6 months after birth) · Cardiac surgery Complications: Delayed brain development · Speech delays · Learning disability · Attention deficit- hyperactivity disorder · Autism spectrum disorder · Psychiatric disorders · Autoimmune disorders · Graft-versus-host disease – T cells attack
:::

:::slides Slide 6 · Assess, act, teach, evaluate
Assessment and analysis: Disturbance of normal embryonic development – thymus – parathyroid gland – facial structures – cardiac structures Nursing diagnoses: Risk for complications – opportunistic infections · Risk for infection – compromised host defense · High risk for ineffective airway clearance – laryngospasm related to hypocalcemia Assessments: Vital signs – T · Lung sounds · Calcium levels – presence of hypoparathyroidism · Tetany · Monitor WBCs Actions: Administer calcium supplements as ordered · Infection control precautions and standard precautions · Strategies for addressing feeding difficulties · Coordinate care for cardiac, infectious disease specialists, and speech pathologists Teaching: Overview of the disease process · Growth and development milestones – early assessment for learning disabilities · Emotional support and networking Evaluating care outcomes: Limited infections · No seizures · Growth and development within normal limits · Support from interprofessional team
:::

:::slides Slide 7 · CMC, Hyper-IgM, SCID, Wiskott–Aldrich
Other T-Cell Immune Deficiencies: Chronic Mucocutaneous Candidiasis – involves thymus and other endocrine glands. Recurrent Candida infections of the skin, nails, mucous membranes. High morbidity due to endocrine dysfunction. Hypofunction of the adrenal cortex is the major cause of death · Hyper- IgM Syndrome – X-linked immunodeficiency with low IgG and IgA serum levels Primary Immune Deficiency of Both T Cells and B Cells: Severe combined immune deficiency (SCID) – there are 13 different genetic defects · Wiskott–Aldrich syndrome – combined B & T cell defect, characteristics include recurrent infection, eczema, and thrombocytopenia
:::

:::slides Slide 8 · Therapy-induced immunodeficiency
Epidemiology: Caused by medication-induced immunosuppression, radiation, or surgery Pathophysiology: Cytotoxic medications destroy WBCs – remaining lymphocytes cannot release antibodies and lymphokines · Corticosteroids interfere with cell-mediated immunity or production of antibodies – decreased T cells (keep T cells in bone marrow) – suppression of cell-mediated immunity and lymphopenia – decreased IgG production – decreased antibody-antigen binding Medical Management: Prevention · Hand washing · Avoiding contact with known infectious persons · Regular provider follow up · Prompt action when signs of infection develop Assessment and analysis: Protect patient from infection · Assess for signs/symptoms of infection Nursing diagnoses: Risk for complications · Risk for infection Assessments: Vital signs – T, hypotension, tachycardia · Breath sounds – may be decreased or adventitious · Urine – cloudy, foul smell – UTI · Skin rashes or lesions Actions: Practice good hand washing · Treat infections as ordered · Anticipate changes in immunosuppressive medications Teaching: Avoid crowds or large gatherings · Do not change cat litter boxes – avoid toxoplasmosis exposure · Avoid turtles or reptiles as pets – salmonella exposure · Eat a low bacteria diet · Report any signs/symptoms of infection Evaluating care outcomes: Takes precautions to prevent infection · Practices good hand washing · Knows when to contact provider · Treats infections promptly
:::

:::slides Slide 9 · When the immune system overreacts
Excessive Immune Response: Autoimmunity – body initiates an immune response against self; body fails to recognize normal cells and causes immune reaction against the perceived antigen · Hypersensitivity reaction – immune response is overactive. An antigen stimulates an immune response in a susceptible individual
:::

:::slides Slide 10 · Immediate reactions & anaphylaxis
Epidemiology: Genetic predisposition – 80% chance if both parents have allergies · Allergic rhinitis most common type I hypersensitivity reaction · Incidence of anaphylaxis not known Pathophysiology [image]: Allergic reaction cycle. (1) First exposure: initial contact with the allergen → (2) B cell becomes a plasma cell and releases IgE antibodies → (3) IgE attaches to receptors on mast cells → (4) Second exposure: the allergen binds the IgE on the mast cell → (5) mast cell degranulates, releasing histamine and other chemicals → allergic reaction.

Clinical Manifestations: Nasal discharge · Sneezing · Pruritus of upper airways · Headache · Sinus pressure · Itchy, watery eyes · Anaphylaxis: systemic response – dyspnea, wheezes/crackles, skin reaction, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, anxiety, flush of heat, angioedema, bronchospasm, hoarseness, and stridor Diagnosis: WBC with differential – reveals increase in eosinophils · Skin testing – scratch test · Glucocorticoids, antihistamines and NSAIDs are discontinued 5 days prior to testing Treatment: Identification, treatment, prevention of allergic responses · Antihistamines and decongestants – diphenhydramine hydrochloride · Steroids · Beta-agonist bronchodilators
:::

:::slides Slide 11 · Assess, act, teach, evaluate
Assessment and analysis: Bronchoconstriction · Increased capillary permeability · Runny nose, itchy, red eyes, rash · Dyspnea · Wheezes Nursing diagnosis: Risk for complications of allergic reaction Assessments: Monitor respiratory rate and depth, lung sounds, pulse ox, and ABG · Monitor blood pressure and pulse · General systems assessment Actions: Discontinue offending agent · Administer oxygen as ordered · Elevate head of bed for ventilation; do not compromise BP · Administer medications as ordered Teaching: Identification of potential causes and avoidance · Signs and symptoms of initial reaction · EpiPen use · Medic Alert bracelet Evaluating care outcomes: Free of symptoms · Normal oxygen level · No evidence of rash, itching, or difficulty breathing
:::

:::slides Slide 12 · Cytotoxic reactions
Type II Hypersensitivity Reaction: Cytotoxic: Complement and antibody-mediated cell destruction · Complement and antibody receptor-mediated inflammation · Antibody-mediated cellular dysfunction Epidemiology: Erythroblastosis fetalis – Rh desensitization; 1 in 1,000 births to Rh-neg women – due to incompatibility · Goodpasture's syndrome – produced antibodies attack the lungs & kidneys – 1 case per one million people per year · Myasthenia gravis – 2 out of 100,000 people – cell dysfunction in the transmission of nerve impulse Subtype 1: Complement is not working · Susceptible to autoimmune diseases · Examples: blood transfusion reactions · hemolytic disease due to blood type or Rh incompatibility · certain medication reactions

[Image: Compatibility of Blood Types] Diagram: type O (top) has no antigens and both A and B antibodies; type AB (bottom) has A and B antigens and no antibodies. Green arrows (compatible) run from O to every type and from A and B to AB. Red crossed arrows (incompatible) run between A and B.

Subtype 2: Goodpasture's syndrome: immune system attacks Goodpasture's antigen in the glomerular basement membrane · Deposits of IgG form along basement membrane of lungs or kidneys · Glomerulonephritis and hemorrhage of lung Subtype 3: Antibodies bind to cell-surface receptors · Myasthenia gravis – the autoantibodies either block the action of acetylcholine or destroy the receptors Medical Management – Treatment: Removal of medication or blood product causing reaction · Plasmapheresis · Corticosteroids · Immunosuppressive medications · IVIG · Dialysis · Renal transplant Complications: Renal failure · Hemolytic reaction · Lung damage · Anemia, leukopenia, or thrombocytopenia · Hemorrhage
:::

:::slides Slide 13 · Immune complex reactions
Immune complex-mediated reactions: Categories: systemic or local immune complex reactions · Examples: Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) · Rheumatoid arthritis · Serum sickness – allergic reaction to meds with protein · Arthus reaction – local immune complex – acute inflammatory response after vaccination Epidemiology: SLE – genetic, environmental, or unknown factors – 53 per 100,000 people – women 9× more than men · Rheumatoid arthritis – women 2.5× more than men · Arthus reaction – rare, no specific prevalence Local Immune Complex Reactions: Deposit of immune complexes in dermal vessels · Arthus reaction is a localized vasculitis due to deposit of immune complexes in dermal vessels after intradermal or subcut injections – raised red lesion develops after 4–10 hours, an ulcer forms in the center Medical Management: Removal of offending agent · Symptom management · Epinephrine or systemic corticosteroids
:::

:::slides Slide 14 · Delayed reactions & latex allergy
Epidemiology: Delayed hypersensitivity – poison ivy – Mantoux test for tuberculosis – latex allergy Pathophysiology and Clinical Manifestations: Antibody independent · Occurs 24–72 hours after exposure · Sensitized T lymphocytes attack antigens and release cytokines · Macrophages responsible for tissue destruction · Range from local dermatitis to anaphylactic shock Medical management: Prevention · Antihistamines · Steroidal skin creams Assessment and analysis: Tissue damage related to inflammatory response – local skin reactions – conjunctivitis – rhinitis – respiratory distress Nursing diagnoses: Risk for localized tissue damage · Risk for impaired oxygenation Assessments: Vital signs – respiratory distress, hypotension, tachycardia · Skin · Previous history of latex allergy · History of repeated surgical procedures · Allergies – avocado, chestnut, mango, papaya, peach, banana, kiwi, raw potato, tomato, passion fruit Actions: Eliminate exposure Teaching: Avoid exposure to items commonly made of latex · Medic Alert bracelet · EpiPen use Evaluating Care Outcomes: Heightened awareness of products that contain latex · EpiPen · Medic Alert bracelet
:::

:::slides Slide 15 · Stimulatory reactions
Type V Hypersensitivity: Stimulated: Antibody-mediated cellular dysfunction · Excess stimulation of normal cell- surface receptor by antibody (stimulatory reaction) · Example: Graves' disease – antibody binds to the TSH receptor sites on the thyroid gland
:::

:::slides Slide 16 · Samuel Smith, 50: anaphylaxis to penicillin
Episode 1: Samuel Smith is a 50-year-old male patient who presents in the emergency department with a temperature of 101°F (38.3°C), a productive cough, fatigue, and difficulty breathing. He has a history of seasonal allergic rhinitis and mild asthma but no other significant health history. He does not report any known medication allergies. Initial orders include: chest radiography · complete blood count · complete metabolic profile · pulse oximetry · sputum for culture and sensitivity.

Episode 2: Mr. Smith's chest radiograph reveals pneumonia. He is placed on IV penicillin. The nurse returns to check on the patient and finds he is short of breath and extremely anxious about his symptoms and states that his skin is "itching" and he feels dizzy. Upon initial assessment, his pulse is rapid, his lips are blue tinged, a rash is apparent on his chest and arms, and wheezing is heard on auscultation of his lungs. The patient expresses a sense of impending doom. Vital signs: BP 85/50 mmHg · HR 130 bpm · pulse oximetry 89%.

Wrap-up: On auscultation, audible wheezing with decreased breath sounds in the lower lobes is noted. Mr. Smith complains of severe dyspnea and shortness of breath. Hoarseness is evident. Itchy skin wheals or hives have spread over his body, forming large, red blotches. Angioedema of his lips and mouth is visible. The nurse immediately discontinues the IV penicillin. The IV tubing is changed, and normal saline is initiated. Epinephrine 0.5 mg is administered IM stat. Oxygen therapy is provided via a 100% high-flow nonrebreather face mask. Oxygen saturation is monitored via pulse oximetry. Mr. Smith is placed on a cardiac monitor, which reveals a heart rate of 130 bpm with occasional premature ventricular contractions (PVCs). His BP is 85/50 mm Hg. His oxygen saturation improves to 90% after the initiation of oxygen. Diphenhydramine 50 mg is administered via IV push, and IV fluids are initiated. Albuterol, a beta agonist, is administered via nebulizer to relieve the bronchospasm. Emergency equipment for endotracheal intubation and possible tracheotomy is readied. Mr. Smith's condition begins to improve. His shortness of breath lessens, and pulse oximetry indicates improved oxygenation at 95%. Vital signs: BP 110/60 · HR 90.
:::
