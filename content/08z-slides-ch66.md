:::slides Slide 1 · What BPH is
Epidemiology: 50% of men aged 51–60 · 90% of men over age 80 Pathophysiology: Enlargement of the prostate – size of a walnut

[Image: Prostate] Side-by-side drawing: a normal prostate sits under the bladder with the urethra running freely through it; an enlarged prostate (BPH) bulges and squeezes the urethra, narrowing the channel urine flows through.

Clinical Manifestations: Difficulty starting flow of urine · Weak urine stream · Multiple interruptions during urination · Dribbling once urination is complete · Urgency, frequency · Nocturia · Bladder outlet obstruction
:::

:::slides Slide 2 · Finding it and the first-line approach
Medical Management – Diagnosis: Digital rectal examination · Urinalysis · Prostate-specific antigen Medical Management – Treatment: Watchful waiting/active surveillance · Avoid tranquilizers and decongestants

[Image: Treatment Options for Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia] Flowchart: patient with lower urinary tract symptoms → history, physical, DRE, urinalysis (± PSA) → AUA symptom score. Mild symptoms → watchful waiting. Moderate to severe → discuss treatment options (medications, minimally invasive therapy, or surgery). Obstructive complications (retention, recurrent UTI, stones, kidney damage) → surgery.
:::

:::slides Slide 3 · Drugs & alternatives
Medical Management – Medications: 5-alpha reductase inhibitors · Alpha-adrenergic blockers · Anticholinergic
:::

:::slides Slide 4 · Procedures
Surgical Management: TURP · Transurethral incision of prostate · Open prostatectomy · Laser surgery

[Image: Transurethral Resection of the Prostate (TURP)] Before: the enlarged prostate narrows the urethra under the bladder and a resectoscope is passed up through the penis. After: the entire inner prostate is removed, leaving only the outer layer, so the channel is wide open and urine flows easily.
:::

:::slides Slide 5 · Assess, act, teach, evaluate
Assessment and analysis: Clinical manifestations are caused by obstruction of urine flow due to an enlarged prostate Nursing diagnoses: Disturbed sleep pattern · Risk for infection Assessments: Urinary symptoms · Temperature · Focused abdominal examination · Bladder scan · Urinalysis Actions: Check for PVR · Catheterization · Administer medications as ordered – 5 alpha reductase inhibitors to limit the production of DHT, a substance that encourages the prostate to hyperplasia; Alpha adrenergic blockers to relax smooth muscles Teaching: Watchful waiting – recognition of worsening symptoms · Decreased liquid intake in the evening · Medication therapy education · Follow-up · Surgical options · Post surgical care Evaluating care outcomes: Less urinary retention · Strong urine stream · Decreased nocturia
:::

:::slides Slide 6 · Epidemiology, risk, pathophysiology, signs
Epidemiology: One of the most commonly diagnosed cancers in men, globally · 5 year survival rate · Diagnosed at age 65 Risk Factors: Family history · Diet high in red meat (heterocyclic amines) · Consumption of calcium · High BMI Pathophysiology: Slow growing and asymptomatic until advanced · Metastasis to lymph nodes, bone, rectum, and bladder

[Image] Normal prostate next to a prostate with cancer: the tumor sits in the outer edge of the gland, away from the urethra, so urine flow isn't blocked early.

Clinical Manifestations: Trouble urinating · Weak stream of urine
:::

:::slides Slide 7 · PSA, biopsy, gleason, TNM
Medical Management – Diagnosis: Digital rectal exam – age 50 (ACS) · Prostate-specific antigen – gold standard · Biopsy

[Image: Gleason Scoring System] Five panels of prostate tissue. Grade 1: small, uniform, well-formed glands that look nearly normal. Grades 2–4: glands become more irregular, crowded, and fused. Grade 5: sheets of cells with almost no gland structure (poorly differentiated).

[Image: Stages of Prostate Cancer] Four drawings of the prostate under the bladder. T1: tiny tumor you can't feel. T2: tumor still inside the prostate. T3: tumor breaks through the capsule into surrounding tissue. T4: tumor invades neighboring organs (bladder, rectum).
:::

:::slides Slide 8 · Radiation, freezing, hormones, chemo, surgery
Medical Management – Treatment: Radiation – non surgical option · Brachytherapy – radioactive seeds · Cryotherapy – liquid nitrogen (freeze the prostate) · Ablative hormone therapy – used in men metastasized to lymph nodes – suppression of testosterone that promotes tumor · Chemotherapy – hormone refractory Surgical Management: Radical prostatectomy – prostate and seminal vesicles are removed – almost always results in impotence · (new) Nerve sparing prostatectomy – attempts to keep the nerves intact that control erection
:::

:::slides Slide 9
Assessment and analysis: Clinical manifestations evident once disease is advanced related to obstruction of flow of urine and mass causing pain Nursing diagnoses: Risk for infection · Sexual dysfunction Assessments: Risk factors · PSA levels – 4 ng/ml requires further assessment · DRE results – large palpable prostate · Urinary symptoms Actions: Administer medications as ordered · Postoperative surgical care Teaching: Disease process · Brachytherapy · Chemotherapy/radiation · Signs of infection · Prevention Evaluating care outcomes: Normal sexual functioning · Normal urination · No incontinence
:::

:::slides Slide 10 · The young man's cancer
Epidemiology: Increased incidence over last 20 years · Due to environmental · Genetics · Increased risk in males with Down's syndrome Risk Factors: Cryptorchidism – greatest risk · incomplete descent of one or both testes from the abdomen into the inguinal canal resulting in absence of scrotum Pathophysiology: Begin in testicles · Spread through blood supply, tubes responsible for sperm transport, lymph system

[Image] Cross-section of a testis inside the scrotum with a distinct mass growing within the testicle.

Clinical Manifestations: Painless mass · Some patients have abdominal dull pain, dull ache · Feeling firm or heavy in the scrotum · The mass may also present with swelling and redness Diagnosis: Physical assessment · Ultrasonography · Laboratory results – alpha-fetoprotein and human chorionic gonadotropin Treatment: Removal of affected testicle for seminomas · Radiation · Chemotherapy Assessment and analysis: Clinical manifestations due to mass or lump in testes · Abdominal pain · Firm or fullness in the scrotum Nursing diagnoses: Ineffective health maintenance · Risk for infection Assessments: Physical assessment – lumps, redness · Vital signs – hypotension, tachycardia, increased temperature Actions: Wound care · Administer chemotherapeutic agents as ordered Teaching: Signs of infection · Chemotherapy · Retroperitoneal lymph node resection – possible nerve damage · Sperm storage – preserve potential fertility · Follow-up Evaluating care outcomes: Early diagnosis · Cure of disease · Successful family planning
:::

:::slides Slide 11 · Rare, spreads easily
Epidemiology: 100 times less common than female breast cancer · 2,360 cases in 2014 Risk Factors: Advanced age between 60 and 70 · Mutation BRCA2 gene or BRCA1 gene · Family history · Excessive alcohol consumption · Testicular disorders · Environmental factors · Hormonal imbalances Pathophysiology: Uncontrolled growth of abnormal cells of breast tissue · Clinical manifestation – Mass in breast tissue Diagnosis: Clinical breast examination · Ultrasonography · MRI Treatment: Surgical resection · Chemotherapy · Radiation · Targeted therapy Assessment and analysis: Clinical manifestations due to mass or lump in breast tissue – lump, swelling, dimpling, nipple discharge, inverted nipple, red warm and swollen breast Nursing diagnoses: Ineffective health maintenance · Risk for infection Assessments: Lump in breast · Dimpling or discharge from nipple · Nipple inversion · Redness · Postoperative assessment Actions: Administer medications as ordered · Change dressing · Empty drain at surgical site · Administer pain medication per order Teaching: Care of suture site · Signs and symptoms of infection · Breast self-examination · Follow-up appointment · Provide emotional support and resources Evaluating care outcomes: Good surgical site healing · Emotional healing
:::

:::slides Slide 12 · Blunt, penetrating & degloving
Epidemiology: 85% from blunt trauma – sports injuries, kick to the groin, MVA, straddling of an object, falls, injury (25–40 years old) · 15% from penetrating trauma Surgical Management: Primary closure using remaining scrotal skin
:::

:::slides Slide 13 · Scott Andrews, 65, BPH
Episode 1: Scott Andrews is a 65-year-old male patient who presents to his healthcare provider with a complaint of difficulty urinating. He complains of pain with urination and occasional blood-tinged urine. He states that he has an increased urgency to urinate and wakes up three to four times during the night to urinate. He also complains of occasional incontinence.

Wrap-Up: After assessment of his symptoms and education, Scott agrees to a blood draw for PSA levels, a urinalysis, and a DRE. Based on the results of the DRE, a PSA of less than 2 ng/mL, and urinalysis within normal limits, Scott is told the best intervention at this time is "watchful waiting." He is told that if his symptoms worsen, he may be prescribed medication therapy such as 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors or alpha-adrenergic blockers. The nurse provides patient education focusing on watchful waiting and the importance of future provider follow- up.
:::
