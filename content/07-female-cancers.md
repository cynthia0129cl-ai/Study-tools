## Breast cancer · 2nd leading cause of cancer death in women

:::matters Why it matters and risk factors
- The 2nd most common cause of cancer death in females. About 2.3 million new cases worldwide (2022). In the US, about 321,910 invasive and 60,730 in situ cases. About 2,670 cases in men.
- **THE single greatest risk factor = AGE.** People aged 80–85 have 15× the risk of those aged 30–35. Black women <50 have a higher risk. Black women have higher breast cancer mortality.
- **More lifetime ovulatory cycles:** early menarche and late menopause raise risk 30–50%.
- **No pregnancy or live birth before 30** nearly doubles the risk. Only a live birth lowers it.
- **BRCA1 and BRCA2** cause 5–10% of breast cancers. Carriers have a 50–85% lifetime breast risk and a 45% ovarian risk by age 70. 70–80% of breast cancers occur by chance.
- Proliferative benign disease (hyperplasia) raises risk up to 70%. Other risks: excessive alcohol, inactivity, obesity, exogenous hormones.
- **BRCA testing** is only for high-risk patients: breast cancer before 50, triple-negative cancer, ovarian cancer, bilateral disease, a relative diagnosed before 45.
:::

:::patho Patho and in-situ lesions
Cells grow and divide abnormally and rapidly, invade nearby tissue, and spread through blood and lymph. Staged by size, nodes, and metastasis. The 2018 AJCC system also uses grade, hormone receptor (ER/PR) status, HER2 status, and Oncotype DX.
- **DCIS** (ductal carcinoma in situ) is **stage 0**, confined to the lining of the milk ducts. It **requires SURGERY**.
- **LCIS** (lobular carcinoma in situ) is in the milk-producing lobules. It is **no longer considered cancer** and has **NO treatment**. The focus is risk reduction and surveillance (mammogram).
- Both DCIS and LCIS increase the risk of later invasive cancer.
:::

### Breast cancer staging

| Stage | Tumor / nodes / mets |
|---|---|
| 0 | In situ (DCIS). No nodes, no mets |
| IA | Tumor up to 2 cm. No nodes |
| IB | No tumor or <2 cm, with node involvement <2 mm |
| IIA | No tumor with nodes >2 mm, OR <2 cm with nodes, OR 2–5 cm with no nodes |
| IIB | 2–5 cm with nodes, OR >5 cm with no nodes |
| IIIA | Any size with 4–8 nodes, or >5 cm with nodes |
| IIIB | Spread to the chest wall, 9 nodes, or inflammatory breast cancer |
| IIIC | Chest wall with ≥10 nodes |
| IV | Any size, nodes, AND metastasis |

![Four stages of breast cancer. I: ≤2 cm, no node spread · II: larger, may reach nearby nodes · III: may involve nodes, chest wall, or skin · IV: any size with distant metastasis](img/s-053-020.png)

:::ss Clinical manifestations ★
- **New mass or lump:** cancerous masses are typically **HARD, IRREGULAR, PAINLESS** and often immovable, but they may be round, soft, and tender.
- Change in breast shape or swelling
- **Skin changes:** peeling, flaking, pitting, **DIMPLING**, redness
- **Nipple changes:** inversion, thickening, drainage
- Axillary node enlargement. **Mets go to bone, lung, nodes, liver, and brain.** (Class: "soft tissue such as abdomen and lung are most impacted.")
:::

:::dx Diagnosis, and why
- **Mammography** (screening). Mammogram plus MRI for high-risk patients.
- **Ultrasound** further defines an area seen on mammogram.
- **BIOPSY = DEFINITIVE:** FNA, core needle, vacuum-assisted, or surgical excision (a wire marks a nonpalpable mass).
- **Sentinel node biopsy** (blue dye or radioactive tracer) finds the first node cancer would reach, which determines stage. If it's negative, the full axillary dissection (20–40 nodes) can be skipped.
- Labs: CBC, platelets, LFTs, chest x-ray. Bone and liver scans if skeletal symptoms or abnormal LFTs. CT of the abdomen plus bone scan for stage II or higher.
:::

:::tx Treatment depends on stage
- **Surgery:** **lumpectomy** (breast conservation, removes only the involved tissue plus a margin) or **mastectomy** (modified radical removes the whole breast plus axillary nodes). For early disease the two are equivalent, so the patient chooses.
- **Lumpectomy is ALWAYS followed by radiation.** Order: surgery → chemo → THEN radiation (case study).
- **Chemotherapy:** for tumors ≥1 cm or positive nodes, and offered to all premenopausal patients. **Neoadjuvant** chemo is first line for intermediate and advanced operable cancer and makes breast conservation more likely.
- **Radiation:** after lumpectomy, after mastectomy if ≥4 nodes are positive, for recurrence, for palliation.
- **Targeted therapy:** **HER2+ → trastuzumab (Herceptin).** Also used for hormone-receptor+, BRCA, and triple-negative tumors.
- **Immunotherapy:** pembrolizumab with chemo before surgery for high-risk triple-negative cancer.
- **Endocrine (hormone) therapy** for ER/PR+ tumors, given 5–10 years after chemo: **tamoxifen or aromatase inhibitors.**
- **Locally advanced or inoperable:** neoadjuvant chemo, then modified radical mastectomy, then chemo plus radiation. No immediate reconstruction.
- **Metastatic:** focus on quality and quantity of life; weigh benefit against toxicity.
:::

:::watch Complications and treatment side effects
- **Immediately post-op:** infection, wound dehiscence, PE, pneumonia (immobility). **Bloody drainage SATURATING the dressing is the most concerning wound finding.** Serosanguineous drainage is expected.
- **Long term:** pain, scarring, **LYMPHEDEMA** (arm swelling after axillary nodes are removed), and **shoulder immobility (the most common long-term effect; "frozen shoulder")**.
- **Chemo:** hair loss, N/V, mouth pain, diarrhea or constipation, peripheral neuropathy, rash, nail changes, ↓WBC (fever and infection), premature ovarian failure (bone loss, infertility), later MDS or leukemia.
- **Immunotherapy:** skin rashes, flu-like symptoms, thyroid problems, diarrhea, autoimmune organ damage.
- **Endocrine therapy:** hot flashes, vaginal dryness, ↑osteoporosis, rarely endometrial cancer.
- **Radiation:** redness, patchy desquamation, itching, irritation of the skin (the key thing to monitor). Breast swelling for 6–18 months. **Pneumonitis** (nonproductive cough with an infiltrate, 6 weeks to 6 months later). Late cardiac risk.
:::

:::watch Hormone drugs (Table 65.3) ★
**Tamoxifen (SERM)** blocks estrogen at its receptor. Taken for 5–10 years.
- **Common:** hot flashes, night sweats, irregular bleeding or spotting, ↓libido, vaginal discharge or dryness.
- **Rare but serious:** DVT and PE, uterine/endometrial cancer, stroke, cataracts, bone loss (premenopausal).
- ⚠ **Avoid SSRIs** (they reduce tamoxifen metabolism). **Monitor for endometrial hyperplasia.**

**Aromatase inhibitors (anastrozole, letrozole, exemestane)** block the conversion of androgens to estrogen. Taken for 5–10 years. ⚠ **Osteoporosis and fractures.**

⚠ **Question an order for ORAL CONTRACEPTIVES in a patient with ER+ breast cancer** (case study).

**Trastuzumab:** fatigue, joint and muscle pain, diarrhea, infusion reactions, rash, fever. **Pembrolizumab:** fatigue, muscle pain, itching, rash, diarrhea.
:::

:::nursing Nursing, and why
- **Dx:** pain · risk for infection · altered nutrition · body image disturbance
- **Vital signs:** ↑BP and HR suggest pain or anxiety. ↑Temperature and HR suggest infection.
- Pain scale. Daily weight (nutrition deficit). I&O (tolerance, vomiting).
- **Wound:** redness, warmth, purulent drainage. Radiated skin checks.
- CBC and metabolic panel: chemo lowers WBC, Hgb, and Hct and raises LFTs.
- Mood and nonverbal cues: high risk of depression.
- Give chemo, targeted therapy, pain meds, and antiemetics. **Cough and deep breathe post-op** *→ why: prevents atelectasis and pneumonia.* Keep the wound clean and dry. Encourage verbalization.
- **Teaching:** treatment course · medication and radiation side effects · wound care (report infection or dehiscence) · nutrition · support groups. **Mammography screening:** 40–44 optional annual · 45–54 annual · ≥55 annual or every 2 years while healthy. **Breast self-exam is NOT recommended** (per Ch 65 and the slides). Lifelong follow-up mammograms.
- **Outcomes:** N/V controlled, weight maintained, wound healed, minimal scarring and lymphedema, anxiety and depression managed.
:::

## Ovarian cancer · most deadly gyn cancer, "silent"

:::matters Why it matters
5th most common cancer in females (5th cause of cancer death per the slides). **70–75% are not found until stage III or IV**, which makes it the most deadly gynecologic cancer. Stage I has >73% 5-year survival; stage III is 21% and stage IV is <5%. Uncommon before 40, and most cases occur in the 70s. **Advanced age is the most common risk.**
:::

:::patho Risk vs protection ★
- **PROTECTIVE:** breastfeeding, pregnancy, oral contraceptives, progesterone-only HRT.
- **RISK:** long-term estrogen or estrogen/progesterone HRT, advanced age, family history and BRCA1/2, HNPCC (Lynch), obesity and PCOS (androgens), possibly infertility and ovulation-stimulating drugs.
:::

![Ovarian cancer: cancer tissue on the left ovary](img/s-056-024.png)

:::ss Clinical manifestations (vague) ★
- **Early (often dismissed as nothing):** bloating · pelvic or abdominal pain · early satiety or trouble eating · urinary urgency or frequency. Nonspecific, not noticed until the disease has progressed.
- **Late: ASCITES** (fluid in the abdomen), so **measure abdominal girth.**
- **Priority call-back:** "I have trouble eating because I feel full and bloated."
:::

:::dx Diagnosis
- **NO recommended screening test** for the general population.
- **CA-125** (blood marker) rises in ovarian cancer, BUT is normal in 50% of early cases and also rises with diverticulitis, endometriosis, cirrhosis, and fibroids.
- Pelvic ultrasound or CT to see the ovaries. CA-125 plus ultrasound is used to screen only high-risk patients (BRCA, HNPCC).
- ⚠ **Biopsy of the mass is NOT recommended** *→ why: it could spread the cancer.*
- **Definitive diagnosis = surgical removal of the mass plus pathology.** FIGO staging is based on surgical findings: **I = ovaries · II = pelvis · III = peritoneum and/or nodes · IV = distant.**
:::

:::tx Treatment
- **Surgical staging and DEBULKING** (remove as much tumor as possible) plus peritoneal washings, biopsies, omentectomy, lymphadenectomy, and usually hysterectomy with both ovaries and tubes. Fertility-sparing surgery only in rare early cases.
- **Chemotherapy AFTER debulking** is the standard of care. Given IV, oral, or intraperitoneal. Neoadjuvant chemo is used in select patients.
:::

:::nursing Nursing
- **Dx:** ineffective coping · acute pain (surgery) · altered nutrition
- **Assess:** symptoms, vital signs, **abdominal girth (ascites)**, weight (loss = nutrition; gain = fluid), CA-125 and imaging, chemo side effects (N/V/D, constipation, hair loss, ↓WBC and RBC), anxiety and depression.
- **Actions:** pain meds, chemo, antiemetics (for nutrition). **Teach ALL female patients the symptoms.** Ease chemo side effects. Support groups and counselors.
- **Outcomes:** nausea controlled, weight maintained, body image addressed, end-of-life issues addressed.
:::

## Uterine (endometrial) cancer · most common gyn cancer in the US

:::matters Why it matters and risk
- The **most common gynecologic cancer in the US**, with a 2.6% lifetime risk. Postmenopausal, ages 55–65 (median 60). Rare under 45. 2–5% are hereditary (HNPCC). More common and more fatal in Black women.
- **#1 risk factors: UNOPPOSED ESTROGEN and OBESITY.** Unopposed estrogen means estrogen therapy without progesterone, tamoxifen, obesity, cirrhosis, estrogen-producing tumors, or anovulation. (Ch 13 adds early menarche, late menopause, PCOS, infertility, and diabetes.)
:::

:::patho Patho
Usually arises in the endometrium. ~80% are **endometrioid adenocarcinoma (type I):** estrogen-driven, usually early, 88% survival. **Type II (serous or clear cell)** is aggressive and occurs in older patients with a poor prognosis. **Sarcoma (muscle)** is 3–5%. Metastatic or recurrent disease is incurable (about 1-year median survival).
:::

![Uterine cancer: endometrioid adenocarcinoma (lining) vs uterine sarcoma (muscle)](img/s-057-025.png)

:::ss Clinical manifestations ★
- **ABNORMAL UTERINE / VAGINAL BLEEDING is the MOST COMMON symptom.**
- Any postmenopausal bleeding needs evaluation. Premenopausal irregular or heavy bleeding with risk factors, or that doesn't respond to treatment.
- Ch 13 also lists lower abdominal pain and cramping.
- **Most concerning call from a 60-year-old: "I have vaginal bleeding."**
:::

:::dx Diagnosis
- **Endometrial biopsy:** 99% sensitive for high-grade cancer.
- **Pelvic (transvaginal) ultrasound:** shows a thickened endometrium.
- **D&C** (scrape the lining) if the biopsy is negative but bleeding continues.
:::

:::tx Treatment
- **Surgery:** remove the uterus, fallopian tubes, and ovaries, plus washings and lymphadenectomy. Early stage I is often cured by surgery alone.
- **Progestin therapy** for stage I if surgery isn't possible.
- **Radiation** is the usual adjuvant. Chemo for high-risk or higher-stage disease, with only a modest response.
:::

:::nursing Nursing
- **Dx:** acute pain (surgery) · nausea (chemo) · ineffective coping
- **Assess:** vital signs, daily weight, I&O, pain, abnormal bleeding, age 55–65.
- **Actions:** pain meds, chemo, comfort (antiemetics, salt-water rinses for stomatitis).
- **Teach prevention:** healthy weight and exercise. **Anyone on estrogen who has a uterus needs progesterone too.** Tamoxifen users need monitoring for endometrial hyperplasia. For stomatitis, avoid hot or spicy food.
:::

## Cervical cancer · almost completely preventable

:::matters Why it matters and risk
- Rates have dropped since the Pap smear (mid-20th century) and HPV screening. Most often diagnosed at ages **35–44** (average 50). Rare under 20. More common in rural areas.
- 90% of new cases and deaths are in low- and middle-income countries. It is the 4th most common cause of cancer death in females worldwide.
- **HPV (high-risk subtypes) is THE CAUSE.** Nearly 100% of cervical cancers test positive. HPV risks: early sexual activity, multiple partners, low socioeconomic status, immunosuppression.
- Contributors: HSV-2, smoking, douching, poor nutrition, long-term oral contraceptive use.
- **HPV vaccine at age 11–12 (can start at 9).** It does not treat an existing infection.
:::

:::patho Patho
A slow-developing disease: cervical dysplasia (low grade, then high grade) becomes invasive only if untreated. Catching dysplasia early prevents cancer.
- **90% are squamous cell carcinomas**, arising in the **transformation zone** where the endocervix meets the ectocervix.
- Most of the rest are adenocarcinomas from the glandular cells of the endocervix.
:::

:::ss Clinical manifestations ★
- **Dysplasia has NO symptoms** and usually can't be seen.
- **Vaginal bleeding is the most common symptom** of invasive cancer. **Postcoital bleeding** (especially).
- Ch 13 adds vaginal discomfort, malodorous discharge, and pain with intercourse.
- Women who are not sexually active may have no symptoms until very advanced.
:::

:::dx Screening and diagnosis ★ (ACOG / ASCCP / SGO)
- **No screening before age 21. First Pap is at age 21.**
- **Ages 21–29:** Pap every 3 years. HPV testing is not a screen in this age group, only a reflex test if atypical cells are found. Primary HPV screening may start at 25 and repeat every 5 years. (ACS prefers starting at 25 with primary HPV.)
- **Ages 30–65:** primary HPV or Pap plus HPV cotest every 5 years, OR Pap alone every 3 years.
- HIV, immunosuppression, DES exposure, or prior dysplasia may need more frequent screening.
- Hysterectomy for a benign cause with no CIN 2 history: stop. Over 65 with normal results: may stop.
- **Abnormal result plus high-risk HPV leads to colposcopy with biopsy** (acetic acid or Lugol's). **Biopsy is diagnostic.**
- Before treatment: MRI (best for tumor volume), PET (distant mets), CT, chest x-ray, IV pyelogram. FIGO staging I–IV.
:::

![Colposcopy: the scope looks through a speculum at the cervix, which has been swabbed with acetic acid, and abnormal areas are biopsied](img/s-058-026.png)

:::tx Treatment depends on stage
- **Early:** cone biopsy, or **radical trachelectomy** (removes the cervix only) for a patient who wants to keep her fertility. If fertility isn't desired: simple or radical hysterectomy with lymph node dissection.
- **Chemoradiation for higher stages:** chemo makes the cells more sensitive to radiation. Radiation is usually **brachytherapy** (internal, placed in or next to the tumor) ± external pelvic beam.
- **Recurrence confined to the pelvis: pelvic exenteration** (all pelvic organs removed) can be curative. Mortality about 5% and morbidity about 70%, leaving a colostomy and urinary diversion. Already radiated? Then chemo is the only option.
- **5-year survival:** localized 92%, regional 58%, distant 17%.
:::

:::nursing Nursing
- **Dx:** ineffective health maintenance (no Pap screening) · ineffective coping · acute pain · nausea (chemoradiation)
- **Focus = PREVENTION** (screening plus HPV vaccine) because there are no symptoms until it's advanced.
- **Assess:** vital signs, Pap and HPV results, I&O, daily weight, pain.
- **Actions:** refer for colposcopy and biopsy, give meds and antiemetics, support and resources.
- **Teach:** routine screening, HPV vaccination for eligible patients, support groups. **Transgender men still need breast, Pap, and pelvic screening.**
:::

## Female cancers: tell them apart ★

| | Breast | Ovarian | Uterine / endometrial | Cervical |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Typical age** | Risk rises with age (Black women <50 at higher risk) | 70s (rare <40) | 55–65, postmenopausal | 35–44 |
| **Biggest risk** | Age. More ovulatory cycles, BRCA, no live birth before 30 | BRCA, family history, HNPCC, HRT, age | Unopposed estrogen and OBESITY (tamoxifen too) | HPV |
| **Protective** | Live birth | Pregnancy, breastfeeding, OCPs | Progesterone with estrogen, healthy weight | HPV vaccine, screening |
| **Key symptom** | Hard, irregular, painless, fixed lump, dimpling, nipple inversion or discharge | Vague: bloating, early satiety, pelvic pain, urinary frequency, late ascites | Abnormal or postmenopausal bleeding | Often none. Vaginal and postcoital bleeding |
| **Screening** | Mammogram (BSE not recommended) | NONE (CA-125 plus ultrasound only if high risk) | None. Counsel at menopause to report bleeding | Pap ± HPV from 21 |
| **Definitive dx** | Biopsy. Sentinel node for staging | Surgical removal plus pathology (no biopsy) | Endometrial biopsy, ultrasound, D&C | Colposcopy plus biopsy |
| **Main treatment** | Lumpectomy plus radiation, or mastectomy. Chemo, targeted therapy, hormones | Debulking, then chemo | Remove the uterus, tubes, and ovaries, then radiation ± chemo | Trachelectomy or hysterectomy, chemoradiation (brachytherapy) |
| **Signature watch** | Lymphedema, shoulder immobility, radiation skin | Ascites (girth), chemo toxicity, end of life | Bleeding, nutrition | Prevention, nausea |
