# Pain Management
@part: Part 9 · Ch 11
@id: pain
@summary: Acute vs chronic, pain types, OPQRST-AAA, acetaminophen, NSAIDs, opioids, adjuvants, older adults.

Pain is **"an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage"** (IASP 2020), and it is **whatever the patient says it is** (McCaffery). More than two-thirds of cancer patients have moderate to severe pain.

## Acute vs chronic

| | Acute | Chronic |
|---|---|---|
| **Time** | Sudden, short (hours to weeks), linked to tissue injury | Lasts more than 3–6 months, may have no clear injury |
| **Purpose** | Protective | Serves no biological purpose |
| **Feels like** | Sharp, easy to pinpoint | Achy, dull, or burning, hard to localize. Resists treatment |
| **Examples** | Post-op, trauma | Migraine, low back pain, neuropathy, fibromyalgia, phantom limb |

- **Gate control theory:** the spinal cord "opens or closes the gate" to pain signals, and the brain shapes how pain is perceived.
- **Peripheral and central sensitization** amplify pain and can make it chronic (synaptic "cellular memory").

## Pain by origin
- **Nociceptive:** cutaneous (skin) and somatic (bone, joint, muscle).
- **Visceral:** hollow organs (heart, bladder, GI). Hard to localize, often referred. **Autonomic signs:** sweating, pallor, nausea, low HR and BP, syncope.
- **Neuropathic:** from nerve damage. Shooting, stinging, "pins and needles" with numb areas. Examples: postherpetic neuralgia, phantom limb, diabetic and chemo-induced neuropathy. **The hardest type to treat.**
- **Older adults** may show atypical pain: confusion, restlessness, poor appetite. Up to 30% have no pain with an MI.

## Assessment: OPQRST-AAA ★

| | |
|---|---|
| **O** Onset | When did it start, sudden or gradual |
| **P** Provocation | What makes it worse (movement, turning) |
| **Q** Quality | Sharp, dull, burning, stabbing, crushing |
| **R** Region / Radiation | Point to it, does it spread |
| **S** Severity | 0–10 scale, effect on function |
| **T** Time / duration | How long, has it happened before |
| **AAA** | **A**ggravating and **A**lleviating factors, **A**ssociated symptoms |

("What makes the pain worse?" assesses **aggravating factors.**)

- The 0–10 scale measures **intensity only**, and scores of **4 or higher** usually prompt intervention. Also assess function. Physical exam: palpation, ROM, neuro checks.
- **Cognitively impaired patients:** attempt self-report first, assume pain if a painful condition exists, establish a behavioral baseline (guarding, rubbing, rocking, grimacing), use **PAINAD** or the Checklist of Nonverbal Pain Indicators, give scheduled nonopioids, and avoid psychotropics (they blunt pain behaviors).

## Nonopioid analgesics

:::watch Acetaminophen
- Analgesic with few anti-inflammatory effects. **Max 1,000 mg per dose and 3,000 mg in 24 hours.**
- ⚠ **Liver damage.** Watch combination products (Percocet, cold medicines) and check labels. Alcohol and barbiturates increase hepatotoxicity. Monitor AST, ALT, and bilirubin.
:::

:::watch NSAIDs and aspirin
- Block COX and prostaglandins (**anti-inflammatory**).
- ⚠ **COX-1 inhibition causes GI ulcers and bleeding**, even short term.
- ⚠ **Renal problems** (caution in CKD and older adults). Avoid in the 3rd trimester.
- ⚠ **Aspirin inhibits platelets**, causing bleeding and thrombocytopenia.
- ⚠ **OTC NSAIDs increase warfarin's effect** (warfarin teaching).
- **COX-2 selective (celecoxib):** ⚠ MI and stroke risk (Vioxx was withdrawn).
- **Older adults (Beers):** avoid chronic NSAIDs (GI bleed risk, especially over 75 or with anticoagulants or steroids). Avoid NSAIDs in symptomatic heart failure.
- **Ketorolac:** bleeding risk with anticoagulants, renal impairment with ACE inhibitors.
:::

## Opioids

:::watch Opioids
- For **moderate to severe acute pain** (post-op, trauma). Less effective for chronic pain and should not be started for it. For acute pain, prescribe short courses (less than 1 week). Work better combined with NSAIDs.
- **Morphine is the gold standard.** Hydromorphone is **5× morphine.** **Fentanyl is about 100× morphine** (IV, or a transdermal patch for chronic cancer pain). Oxycodone (OxyContin) is highly addictive.
- ⚠ **RESPIRATORY DEPRESSION is the most critical adverse effect** (from **mu receptors**). Monitor sedation and respirations, especially after IV doses and in the first 24 hours for opioid-naïve patients.
- ⚠ **Constipation** (mu receptors, ↓GI motility). Stool softeners and laxatives are almost always needed. More common in older adults.
- ⚠ Nausea and vomiting (aspiration risk post-op), urinary retention, pruritus, **confusion and delirium in older adults** (more common than respiratory depression), and paradoxical ↑pain in chronic use.
- **Tolerance** = needing a higher dose for the same effect. **Addiction** = compulsive use despite harm. They are not the same. Fewer than 5% of cancer patients become addicted.
- **PCA:** a low background dose plus patient-controlled boluses within preset limits.
:::

## Adjuvants and other options
- **Corticosteroids:** anti-inflammatory, used as adjuvants in cancer pain.
- **Local anesthetics:** **lidocaine** (onset 10 minutes, lasts 2 hours) and **bupivacaine** (more potent, CNS and cardiac toxicity). ⚠ **Lidocaine with epinephrine is NOT used on fingers, toes, or the penis** (necrosis). **EMLA** is applied 45–60 minutes before a needle stick.
- **Neuropathic pain:** gabapentin, pregabalin, TCAs (in older adults use nortriptyline or desipramine, and get an ECG), duloxetine (SNRI).
- Anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin) can increase warfarin's effect.
- **Ketamine** (NMDA blocker) for resistant pain. Clonidine intrathecal or epidural. Baclofen.
- **Rubefacients and capsaicin: not on radiated skin.**
- **WHO ladder (Ch 26):** nonopioid → moderate opioid plus nonopioid → strong opioid plus nonopioid. **Give around the clock for cancer pain.**
- **Nonpharmacologic:** heat and cold, relaxation, massage, exercise, guided imagery, music, CBT, self-management education.

:::note Class: slide 22
Know why we use opioids vs nonopioids. **Nonopioids** (acetaminophen, NSAIDs) for mild pain and anti-inflammation; **opioids** for moderate to severe pain; in cancer, use the WHO ladder and dose around the clock. Know your pain medications.
:::
