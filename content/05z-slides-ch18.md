:::slides Slide 1 · Innate vs. adaptive immunity
Immunity – refers to the body's ability to resist infection and disease Innate – natural immunity – first line of defense – present at birth, protects the host without remembering prior contact; response without memory Adaptive – is acquired – it is a natural exposure; examples are infection, transfer of maternal antibodies, vaccination, infusion of immune globulin. Both cellular & humoral mediated Components of the immune system: Bone marrow · Lymphoid tissue. Bone marrow: T cells & B cells. T lymphocytes mature in the thymus. B lymphocytes mature in bone marrow. Lymphoid tissue: Spleen & lymph nodes
:::

:::slides Slide 2 · What the immune system does
Function of the Immune system: Removes foreign antigens such as viruses and bacteria to maintain homeostasis · Phagocytosis – monocytes are responsible for engulfing and destroying foreign bodies and toxins · Inflammatory response – to injury or invading organisms; minimize blood loss; activate phagocytosis, formation of scar tissue and regeneration of injured tissue Immunity – Natural immunity: nonspecific response to any foreign invader. WBC action: release cell mediators such as histamine, bradykinin and prostaglandins – engulf foreign substance. Inflammatory response. Physical barriers: intact skin, chemical barriers, acidic secretions, enzymes – tears & saliva Physical, Mechanical, and Biochemical Barriers: Skin · Mucous membranes · Protective lining of organs · Mechanisms – sloughing, sneezing, vomiting, urination Acquired Immunity: is specific against a foreign antigen – result of prior exposure to an antigen – active or passive. Active immunity – develops by person's own body; lasts many years; maybe lifetime
:::

:::slides Slide 3 · The immune response map
[Image: The Immune Response] Flowchart: Immune responses split into innate (natural) immunity (nonspecific) and acquired (adaptive) immunity (specific). Innate → first line of defense (physical, biochemical, mechanical barriers) and second line of defense (inflammatory response). Acquired → third line of defense → naturally acquired (active: infection; passive: maternal antibodies) and artificially acquired (active: vaccination; passive: immune serum).
:::

Lymph nodes and the Lymphatic system: Thymus – located in the chest cavity; stimulates T cells & where they develop · Bone Marrow – B & T lymphocytes · Spleen – part of the lymphatic system; serves as a blood filter for old and damaged RBC Additional Lymphoid tissues: Tonsils – traps bacteria & viruses inhaled · Adenoids – located in nasopharynx, also traps inhaled bacteria & viruses · Peyer's Patches – lymphoid follicles located in the small intestines, known as the intestinal immune sensors against pathogens

[Image: Central and Peripheral Organs of the Immune System] Body figure labeling central organs (thymus, bone marrow) and peripheral organs (adenoids, tonsils, lymph nodes, lymphatic vessels, spleen, Peyer's patches in the small intestine, lymph nodes in the jejunum).

:::slides Slide 5 · Leukocytes (WBCs)
Leukocytes – are the mobile units, travel through the bloodstream to fight infection · Neutrophils – phagocytes – destroy bacteria at early inflammation · Basophils – release heparin as anticoag & histamine during early inflammation · Eosinophils – destroy allergens & combat parasitic infections · Macrophages – digest & destroy microorganisms
:::

:::slides Slide 6 · B cells, t cells & immunoglobulins
Lymphocytes: B Lymphocytes – found in lymph nodes, spleen & thymus – Immunoglobulins (Igs) produce antibodies (IgA, IgD, IgE, IgG and IgM) · T Lymphocytes – Natural killer cells – target and kill cancer cells

Table 18.2 – Immunoglobulins that B cells produce: IgA – found in secretory gland secretions – breast milk, sweat, saliva, mucus and tears · IgD – plays a role in B cell activation · IgE – acts as a defender against parasitic infections; mediator of allergic responses · IgG – transported across placenta & effective against bacteria, viruses & toxins · IgM – first antibody produced during primary response to an antigen

[Image: Process of Differentiation of T-Cell and B-Cell Lymphocytes] Flowchart from bone marrow. Left (humoral immunity): B cells → bone marrow maturation → B lymphocytes → memory cells and plasma cells → antibodies. Right (cellular, cell-mediated immunity): T cells → thymus → regulatory T cells (→ suppressor T cells) and effector T cells (→ helper T cells and cytotoxic T cells).
:::

:::slides Slide 7 · Messengers & helpers
Cont with Cells: Dendritic cells – macrophage cell, aids in T cell & B cell recognition · Mast cells – found in the skin and lining of the respiratory and GI tracts; similar to basophils they release heparin and histamine during early inflammatory response · Cytokines – are small proteins that regulate immune response – Interleukins – suppresses inflammation; stimulates leukocyte production & maturation · Interferon – proteins that protect against viral and tumor growth · Tumor necrosis factor alpha – produce macrophages, involved in production of immune cells
:::

:::slides Slide 8 · The antibody's backup crew
Anatomy of the Immune System: Complement system – is a system of proteins that complement or help the actions of antibodies and other cells in destroying bacteria or microbes · Whenever a pathogen enters the body, it gets recognized by antibodies present in our blood, then the binding of the antibody & pathogen occurs activating the complement system, by different cascades of enzymes killing the microbe
:::

:::slides Slide 9 · The acute inflammatory response
[Image: Acute Inflammatory Response] Flowchart: tissue damage → pathogen invasion → inflammatory response → splits into vasodilation (↑ blood flow, facilitating leukocyte movement [chemotaxis] to the inflamed area) and increased capillary permeability (leukocytes move into the tissue) → invading organisms engulfed and phagocytized + fibrinogen clot border forms → cardinal signs of inflammation: localized redness (erythema), warmth, edema, drainage (serous exudate or pus), pain → systemic response: fever, malaise, chills, elevated WBC count (leukocytosis).
:::

:::slides Slide 10 · Cellular vs. humoral immunity
Adaptive Immune Response: Cellular – mediated by T lymphocytes · Humoral – mediated by the B lymphocytes Cellular Mediated Response: T cells · Direct destruction of foreign or abnormal cells Humoral-Mediated Response: Agglutination – when 2 antigens react – clump – facilitates phagocytosis and enables the body to clear itself of the invading organism · Opsonization – process in which the antigen-antibody binding is coated with a pasty substance
:::

:::slides Slide 11 · Variables, advances & the nurse's role
Variables affecting Immune Function: Age & gender · Nutrition & lifestyle · Hx of infection or immunization · Allergies · Presence of conditions or disorders: autoimmune; cancer; chronic illness; surgery · Medications & transfusions · Psychoneuroimmunologic factors Advances in Immunology: Genetic engineering: DNA technology · Stem cells – research shows that stem cells can restore an immune system that has been destroyed · Clinical trials using stem cells – RA, SLE, scleroderma and multiple sclerosis Nurse's Role in Evaluation of the Immune System: Offer support · Reduce anxiety · Provide patient education and counseling
:::

:::slides Slide 12 · Assessment of the immune system
History – genetics? chem induced? · Age · Current medications – abx, anti-inflammatory · Family history; thyroid suppressive tx · Social history · Nutritional status – adequate vits/min · Infection history · Immunizations – childhood & adult · Chronic illnesses – allergies · Autoimmune disorders – immunosuppressants · Cancer – antineoplastic agents Physical examination: Vital signs, LOC · Inspection – skin (rashes, dermatitis-type lesions, hematoma, petechiae, purpura), mucous membranes, edema, gait, vision, hearing, cardio, respiratory, GI, GU · Auscultation – listen for adventitious sounds, heart sounds, bowel sounds · Palpation and percussion – lymph nodes, hepatosplenomegaly, masses, fluid in abd

:::slides Slide 14 · Immunosenescence
[Image: Age-Related Changes in the Immune System] Flowchart: The Aging Immune System → three branches. (1) Decreased immunologic function; less efficient immune responses → increased infections, especially secondary infections. (2) Increased production of autoantibodies → increased autoimmune disorders. (3) Decline in immune response; malignant cells not destroyed as quickly → increased cancer incidence.

:::slides Slide 15 · Grace Hall, 60: herpes zoster (shingles)
Episode 1: Grace Hall is a 60-year-old female who presents to an urgent care clinic with a 4-day history of low- grade fever, malaise, headache, and an itching and painful sensation on the right side of her chest. She states that she has stopped exercising due to the discomfort and finds tight clothing to be irritating. Her maximum oral temperature has been 100.4°F (38°C). Ms. Hall denies weakness, dizziness, confusion, difficulty swallowing or breathing, cough, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and weight loss. She also denies exposure to ill individuals or recent travel.

Episode 2: The nurse practitioner at the clinic obtains a comprehensive health history and performs a physical examination on Ms. Hall. Her vital signs show her to have a low-grade fever, with a temperature of 100.2°F (37.8°C), a heart rate of 88 bpm, and a blood pressure reading of 128/82 mm Hg. Her examination is significant for an erythematous maculopapular rash with several vesicles on the right upper chest.

Wrap-up: Based on Ms. Hall's history of presenting symptoms and physical examination, she was diagnosed with herpes zoster (shingles). Herpes zoster is a reactivation of the chickenpox or varicella- zoster virus (VZV) that can result in post-herpetic neuralgia. The nurse practitioner instructs Ms. Hall to increase her fluid intake and avoid exposure to individuals who may be immunosuppressed. She is instructed to immediately report if she develops increasing fever or pain. She will follow up every 1 to 2 weeks until resolution of her symptoms.
