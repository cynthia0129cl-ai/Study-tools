Design a teaching plan that includes pharmacological, dietary and lifestyle considerations for patients with reproductive disorders Epidemiology: Second most cause of cancer-related deaths in women · In 2022 – est – 2.3 million new cases diagnosed globally · In US statistics est 321,910 new invasive breast ca cases · Estimated 60,730 new in-situ cases · In men, there are 2,670 est new cases · The single risk factor for breast cancer is woman's age · Black population, younger than 50 has higher risk of developing breast cancer Risk Factors: Higher number of ovulatory cycles in a lifetime · Early menarche and late menopause – 30 to 50% · Lack of pregnancy and live childbirth – 2x the risk · Family Hx with known genetic mutations (BRCA1, BRCA2) Breast Cancer gene – 5 to 10% · Genetic carriers have a 50–80% lifetime risk · Other factors: excessive alcohol use, inactivity, obesity and the use of hormones

:::slides Slide 2 · How it grows and is staged
Pathophysiology: Cells grow and divide abnormally and rapidly · Can invade nearby tissues and spread to other areas through the bloodstream and lymphatic system · Can be categorized or staged according to size, spread to lymph nodes and distant spread to other organs or tissues Breast Cancer Staging: Stage 0 – in situ; no lymph node; no metastasis · Stage IA – tumor size 2 cm; no lymph node; no metastasis · Stage IB – no tumor or tumor size < 2 cm; +lymph node is < 2 mm; no metastasis · Stage IIA – no tumor; or tumor is < 2 cm; or tumor is 2–5 cm; +lymph node > 2 mm; no metastasis · Stage IIB – tumor is 2–5 cm; +lymph node; no metastasis · Stage IIIA – tumor is > 5 cm or tumor of any size; no lymph node for 5 cm but has +4–8 lymph nodes for tumor of any size; no metastasis · Stage IIIB – tumor is > 5 cm or tumor of any size that spread to chest wall; +9 lymph nodes; no metastasis · Stage IIIC – tumor of any size and spread to the chest wall; +10 or more lymph nodes; no metastasis · Stage IV – tumor of any size and has lymph nodes and has metastasized

[Image: Four Stages of Breast Cancer] Four breast drawings. Stage I: small tumor (≤ 2 cm), no node spread.

Stage II: larger tumor, may reach the axillary nodes. Stage III: large tumor spreading to more nodes, chest wall, or skin. Stage IV: any size, spread to distant organs.
:::

:::slides Slide 3 · Signs and how it's diagnosed
Clinical Manifestations: New mass or lump – cancerous masses are typically hard, irregular and painless, maybe round, soft and tender · Changes in breast shape or swelling · Skin changes – peeling, flaking, pitting, dimpling or redness · Nipple change – inversion, thickening of the tissue or drainage Medical and Surgical Management – Diagnosis: Mammography · Ultrasonography · Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) · Mammography with MRI – high risk · Biopsy – definitive results for Dx · Sentinel node biopsy – determine the stage · Labs – CBC, platelets, LFTs, CXR · Bone scan & liver scan – skeletal symptoms · CT abdomen & bone scan – for stage II or higher
:::

:::slides Slide 4 · Surgery, therapies & treatment by stage
[Image: Breast Cancer Surgery] (A) Lumpectomy: only the tumor and a margin of surrounding tissue are removed; the breast stays. (B) Modified radical mastectomy: all breast tissue is removed along with the axillary lymph nodes; the chest muscle stays.

[Image: Mastectomy] Photo of a healed chest after bilateral mastectomy, with flat horizontal scars where the breasts were. Treatment – depends on the stage of the cancer: Surgery (lumpectomy or mastectomy) – Neoadjuvant – before · Chemotherapy – cytotoxic drugs · Radiation – high energy x-rays or particles · Targeted therapy – target genes, proteins or tissue · Immunotherapy – stimulates own body's immune system · Endocrine therapy – blocks the hormones that stimulate cancer growth · Adjuvant therapy Treatment: Lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS) – no treatment · Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) – requires surgery · Early stage invasive ca – surgical excision f/b radiation or chemotherapy; endocrine therapy – tamoxifen · Intermediate stage and advanced stage (operable) – first line of treatment – neoadjuvant chemo f/b radiation; targeted therapy; endocrine therapy – 5 to 10 years · Locally advanced and inoperable ca – neoadjuvant chemo then modified radical mastectomy – post-op chemo & radiation · Local recurrence and systemic metastases – complete mastectomy for those who have undergone conservation therapy, surgical excision, radiation, chemotherapy, or therapeutic benefit has to be weighed
:::

:::slides Slide 5 · After surgery & treatment
Complications – Immediate post op: Infection · Wound dehiscence · Pulmonary embolism · Pneumonia · Pain · Scarring · Lymphedema – removal of axillary node · Shoulder immobility – most common LT effect
:::

:::slides Slide 7 · Assess, act, teach, evaluate
Assessment and Analysis – Initial manifestations: Presence of a tumor – lump in the breast · Skin and nipple changes. Later manifestations: Chemotherapy side effects · Lymph node removal – lymphedema · Immunotherapy – skin rashes, flu-like symptoms, diarrhea · Endocrine therapy – hot flashes, vaginal dryness · Radiation – skin irritation, redness, itchiness, burning · Breast swelling and non-productive cough Nursing Diagnosis: Pain/discomfort · Risk for infection · Nutrition, altered: less than body requirements · Body image disturbance Assessments: Vital signs – elevated BP & HR, T? · Pain · Daily weight – nutritional deficit? · Intake and output · Wound evaluation – early signs of infection? · Monitor laboratory data – CBC & metabolic profile · Assess mood and nonverbal cues – great risk for psychological issues – depression Actions: Give chemotherapeutic drugs & targeted therapy as ordered · Give pain meds · Give antiemetics · Cough and deep breathe post-op · Wound care · Encourage verbalization Teaching: Explain treatment course · Medications and treatment side effects · Wound care · Nutritional counseling · Support groups · Breast cancer screening with mammography · Self-breast examination is not recommended Evaluating care outcomes: Manage nausea and vomiting · Maintain nutrition and weight · Surgical site healing · Minimum scarring and lymphedema · Managed anxiety and depression
:::

:::slides Slide 8 · The silent, deadliest gyn cancer
Epidemiology: 5th cancer cause of death in women · 70%–75% not discovered until late stages III & IV · Hx of breastfeeding, oral contraceptive use has protective effect · Use of progesterone alone as hormone replacement Risk Factors: Protective effect: breastfeeding, pregnancy, oral contraceptives · Hormonal replacement therapy – LT use of progesterone/estrogen increases the risk · Advanced age – mostly in 70's · Family Hx – BRCA1 or BRCA2 gene mutations · Hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer Pathophysiology: Cells divide abnormally and uncontrollably

[Image: Ovarian Cancer] Uterus with fallopian tubes: the right ovary is normal; the left ovary is enlarged and filled with cancerous tissue. Clinical Manifestations – Early stage: Bloating · Pelvic or abdominal pain · Early satiety or problems eating · Urinary urgency or frequency. Later stage: Ascites, excessive fluid in the abdominal cavity Medical Management – No recommended screening test for Ovarian Ca. If suspected: Diagnosis · Biochemical marker CA 125 – blood test – CA 125 levels are elevated (50% in early cases are normal) · Pelvic ultrasonography – visualizes ovaries (biopsy not recommended at this time) · CT scan · Surgical removal of mass and biopsy – definitive Dx Medications: Chemotherapy follows surgical debulking – standard of care Surgical Management: Surgical debulking · Removal of uterus, ovaries, and fallopian tubes
:::

:::slides Slide 9 · Assess, act, teach, evaluate
Assessment and analysis: Clinical signs and symptoms are nonspecific and ovarian cancer is usually not found until the disease is advanced Nursing diagnoses: Ineffective coping · Acute pain · Altered nutrition, less than body requirements Assessments: Signs/symptoms · Vital signs · Abdominal girth – later stage – ascites · Weight · Diagnostic tests · Chemotherapy side effects Actions: Give pain medications as ordered · Give chemotherapeutic agents as ordered · Give antiemetics as ordered Teaching: Signs/symptoms of ovarian cancer · Methods to ease chemotherapy side effects · Support groups or counselors Evaluating care outcomes: Nausea under control · Adequate nutrition to maintain weight · Body image addressed · End of life issues addressed
:::

:::slides Slide 10 · The most common gyn cancer
Epidemiology: 2.6% lifetime risk for women in developed nations · Uterine ca or endometrial ca is the most common gyne ca in the US · Occurs in postmenopausal women – ages 55–65 · 2–5% are hereditary gene Risk Factors: Unopposed estrogen exposure – result of estrogen therapy in the absence of progesterone · Obesity Pathophysiology: Cells of the body grow and divide abnormally and uncontrollably · Cells can invade nearby tissues and spread to other areas through blood and lymph

[Image: Uterine Cancer] Uterus cross-section showing two types: endometrioid adenocarcinoma growing from the inner lining (endometrium) and uterine sarcoma growing in the muscular wall (myometrium).

Clinical Manifestations: Vaginal bleeding – most common symptom Medical Management – Diagnosis: Biopsy · Pelvic ultrasound · Surgical dilation and curettage, scraping the lining of the uterus for tissue sample Medical Management – Treatment: Surgical removal of uterus, fallopian tubes, and ovaries · Radiation · Chemotherapy
:::

:::slides Slide 11 · Assess, act, teach, evaluate
Assessment and analysis: Identification of at-risk patient (age 55–65) and diagnosis imperative to further assessment · Pelvic ultrasound · Endometrial biopsy · Presentation – abnormal uterine bleeding Nursing diagnoses: Acute pain associated to surgical procedure · Nausea associated with chemotherapy · Ineffective coping associated with the cancer diagnosis Assessments: Vital signs · Daily weight · Intake and output · Pain · Abnormal uterine bleeding – most common symptom · Patient age – 55–65 years of age Actions: Administer pain medications as ordered · Administer chemotherapy as ordered · Provide comfort measures Teaching: Educate about disease prevention – obesity & unopposed estrogen are the most common risk factors · Discuss chemotherapy comfort measures · Provide psychological support resources Evaluating care outcomes: Maintain adequate weight · Achieve most daily functions
:::

:::slides Slide 12 · The preventable gyn cancer
Epidemiology: Cervical cancer in women in US has declined since the introduction of the Pap smear in mid 20th century · Introduction of HPV screening in recent years · Common age diagnosed 35–44 years · Low to middle income countries account for new diagnoses and deaths – 90% · 4th most common death in females worldwide Risk Factors: Human papillomavirus (HPV) – cause · Herpes simplex virus type 2 · Smoking · Douching · Low socio-economic status · Poor nutrition · Immunosuppression · Early sexual activity · Multiple partners · Oral contraceptive use Pathophysiology: Slow-developing disease · Early cervical dysplasia or abnormal cell maturation – evolving into invasive cancer if untreated · 90% squamous cells · Cervical cancers are adenocarcinomas originating from glandular cells of endocervix Clinical Manifestations: Vaginal bleeding – most common symptom · Post coital bleeding Medical Management – Diagnosis: Preventable with routine screening (Pap smear) · 1st Pap smear age 21 · Women between 21–30 with normal Pap smears should have every 3 years · Radiological evaluation

[Image: Colposcopy] Side view of the pelvis: a speculum holds the vagina open while a colposcope (a lighted magnifier outside the body) is aimed at the cervix; bladder and uterus labeled. Medical Management – Treatment: Chemoradiation (brachytherapy) – internal radiation placed into or next to the tumor · Chemotherapy Surgical Management: Radical trachelectomy – removal of uterine & cervix only (early stage); for women who desire to maintain fertility · Radical hysterectomy – for women without desire for fertility, a radical hysterectomy with lymph node dissection is performed
:::

:::slides Slide 13 · Assess, act, teach, evaluate
Assessment and analysis: Focus is on prevention (Pap smear) as there are no symptoms until the disease is very advanced Nursing diagnoses: Ineffective health maintenance (lack of Pap screening) · Ineffective coping · Acute pain · Nausea Assessments: Vital signs · Pap smear results – cervical cell change · Intake and output · Daily weight · Pain scale Actions: Refer for colposcopy/biopsy · Give medications as ordered · Give antiemetics as ordered · Give support and offer resources Teaching: Educate on the need for routine screening · Eligible patients need to receive HPV vaccination · Provide information on support group and resources Evaluating care outcomes: Prevention of disease · Pain under control · Nutritional needs met · Psychological support
:::
