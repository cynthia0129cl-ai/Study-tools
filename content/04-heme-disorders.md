# Hematologic Disorders
@part: Part 4 · Ch 34
@id: heme-disorders
@summary: IDA, B12, folate, sickle cell, aplastic, G6PD, PV, thrombocytopenia (ITP, hemophilia, DIC, HIT), leukemia, lymphoma, multiple myeloma.

The four components are RBCs, WBCs, platelets, and plasma. Most disorders come from having too many or too few of one cell type. **Anemia** means reduced O₂-carrying capacity (fewer RBCs or less Hgb).

## Iron deficiency anemia (IDA) · RBC disorder

:::matters Why it matters / risk factors
- The **most prevalent nutritional deficiency in the world**. Seen most in infants, children, pregnant people, and lower socioeconomic groups. More common in females. About 10% more anemia after age 65.
- **Inadequate dietary iron is the #1 cause.** Needs rise during pregnancy and growth.
- **Hemorrhage and chronic blood loss:** heavy menses, GI cancers (esophagus, colon, stomach), peptic ulcer disease.
- **Poor absorption:** celiac, Crohn's, gastric bypass or gastrectomy, long-term PPIs, H2 blockers, antacids, aspirin, NSAIDs.
- **Socioeconomic:** lead paint. **Pica** (craving ice, dirt, clay, paint) usually stops with iron replacement.
- IDA is often one of the earliest signs of another disease (such as GI cancer), so the cause must be worked up. Normal menses should not cause IDA.
:::

:::patho Patho
Iron is needed to make hemoglobin. Without it, Hgb can't form, so not enough O₂ is carried. Iron is stored in the liver as **ferritin** and transported by **transferrin**. IDA means the iron stores are exhausted. It develops slowly in stages: negative iron balance → depletion → deficient RBC production → IDA. **Anemia is a LATE sign** of iron deficiency (except with hemorrhage).
:::

:::ss Clinical manifestations ★
- Hypoxia from low Hgb causes **fatigue, pallor, SOB**.
- **Tachycardia and tachypnea** *→ why: the heart and lungs try to compensate for hypoxemia.*
- **Glossitis:** painful, swollen, smooth, shiny, bright red tongue from flattened papillae. Also cracks at the corners of the mouth.
- **Koilonychia:** spoon-shaped nails after severe, prolonged deficiency.
- Changes in LOC, cognitive impairment (in children it can be permanent), pica.
- **Distinguishing features:** glossitis plus spoon nails, microcytic and hypochromic cells, ↓ferritin with ↑TIBC. **No neuro symptoms** (which separates it from B12 deficiency).
:::

![Koilonychia (spoon-shaped, thin nails) in IDA](img/s-023-015.png)

:::dx Diagnosis
- **CBC:** ↓Hgb and Hct, ↓MCV (microcytic), ↓MCHC (hypochromic). Often the first sign.
- **Iron panel (needed to confirm):** ↓iron, ↓ferritin (stores), transferrin low or normal, **transferrin saturation ALWAYS low, TIBC HIGH** (empty binding spots).
- No single lab makes the diagnosis; it takes the whole picture. Iron and ferritin can fluctuate with inflammation.
- *SLIDE vs BOOK:* slide says ferritin "<100 ug/dL indicates IDA." Book says "serum ferritin <10 mcg/L indicates IDA." Ask which number your instructor wants.
:::

:::tx Treatment
- **Diet first.** Heme iron: red meat, eggs. Non-heme iron: dark green leafy vegetables (spinach, broccoli, peas), beets, dried beans and legumes, nuts, fortified cereals and breads, Cream of Wheat.
- **Vitamin C** (oranges, grapefruit, citrus juice) increases absorption.
- **Oral iron is first line.** Take with citrus juice and **NOT with milk or dairy** *→ why: dairy binds iron and blocks absorption.*
- **Parenteral iron** is for severe GI distress, malabsorption, or when levels must rise fast. **IM iron uses the Z-track method** *→ why: prevents leakage and skin staining.* Old iron dextran was cardiotoxic. Newer IV forms are safer, but still monitor vital signs during the infusion.
:::

:::watch Side effects and complications
- **Oral iron:** nausea, abdominal discomfort, constipation and/or diarrhea. The patient should report these.
- **Complications:** heart failure, renal failure, impaired thermoregulation and immunity, psychomotor and cognitive impairment in children. In pregnancy: low birth weight, preterm labor, maternal or infant death.
- **Iron overload** (from pica or many transfusions, not usually oral pills): fatigue, palpitations, joint pain, GI upset, heart and artery damage.
:::

:::nursing Nursing
- **Dx:** inadequate tissue perfusion · fatigue · activity intolerance
- **Assess:** vital signs (tachycardia and tachypnea are compensation), Hgb and ferritin, fatigue, pallor, SOB, LOC, blood loss, and the cause (diet, IBD, malabsorption).
- **Actions:** ↑dietary iron and vitamin C, give iron supplements, minimize blood loss (control any hemorrhage; hormones for heavy menses).
- **Teach:** iron food sources, take daily supplements as prescribed, report GI side effects, dangers of lead, prenatal iron.
- **Outcomes:** normal Hgb and ferritin, better perfusion, HR and RR back to normal.
:::

## Other RBC disorders: tell them apart ★

| Disorder | Patho / cause | Signature manifestations (beyond fatigue, pallor, ↑HR, ↑RR) | Key labs | Treatment and watch-outs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Iron deficiency** | Iron stores exhausted, so Hgb can't be made | Glossitis, spoon nails, pica | Microcytic, ↓ferritin, ↑TIBC | Iron plus vitamin C. Avoid dairy with doses. Z-track for IM |
| **Vitamin B₁₂ (pernicious)** | B₁₂ needs **intrinsic factor** (from stomach parietal cells) to be absorbed in the **ileum**. Pernicious anemia = autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor. Risks: older adults (gastric atrophy), GI resection, PPIs and H2 blockers, alcohol, vegans and vegetarians (B₁₂ only in animal foods) | **NEURO:** symmetric paresthesias of hands and feet (first neuro sign), **Lhermitte's sign** (electric shock with neck flexion), confusion, depression, dementia, poor balance (especially in the dark), yellow-blue color blindness, tinnitus, glossitis. Pernicious anemia: vitiligo, early gray hair | **Macrocytic (↑MCV).** Serum B₁₂ (200–1,000 pg/mL). **↑MMA** (more sensitive) with normal homocysteine. Schilling test (rare) | Diet (meat, seafood, eggs, dairy) or oral supplement. Severe deficiency needs **weekly B₁₂ injections**. ⚠ Neuro damage can become irreversible. ⚠ **Folic acid can mask B₁₂ deficiency** (fixes the anemia while the nerves keep degenerating). ⚠ Nitrous oxide can cause profound, possibly fatal postop anemia |
| **Folic acid** | Absorbed in the **jejunum**. Alcohol, malnutrition, gastric bypass or Whipple, meds (OCPs, metformin, antineoplastics) interfere | Mood changes, bleeding risk (pancytopenia). **NO lasting neuro deficits**; confusion is reversible | Macrocytic. **↑Homocysteine with NORMAL MMA** (the reverse of B₁₂). Fasting serum folate. RBC folate reflects the last 3 months | Supplement. **≥400 mcg daily in pregnancy prevents neural tube defects.** ⚠ Folic acid speeds phenytoin metabolism and can cause seizures, so correct the deficiency before starting phenytoin |
| **Sickle cell disease** | Genetic **Hgb S**. RBCs sickle with low O₂, becoming stiff and fragile, lodging in capillaries and forming thrombi. RBC life is 15–20 days. **Triggers:** hypoxemia, dehydration, cold, infection, high altitude | Pain (joints, bones, chest, abdomen), swelling, jaundice, splenomegaly, fever, poor wound healing, hand-foot syndrome, **priapism**, infections (pneumococcus, H. influenzae). Symptoms start after 6 months of age (fetal Hgb protects until then) | **Hgb electrophoresis (Hgb S).** ↑bilirubin from hemolysis. ↓retics in aplastic crisis | **Hydroxyurea** (makes fetal Hgb; ⚠ myelosuppression, teratogenic, so counsel on contraception). Transfusions (⚠ iron overload, so give chelation such as desferrioxamine). **Aggressive hydration**, analgesia (often opioids at higher doses), O₂ only if critically low. **Stem cell transplant is the only cure.** Vaccines: pneumococcal and flu. ⚠ **Crises:** vaso-occlusive (most common), aplastic, sequestration (shock), hemolytic. ⚠ Acute chest syndrome, stroke |
| **Aplastic anemia** | Marrow fails (infection, chemicals, myelotoxins), so **ALL 3 cell lines drop (pancytopenia)** | Anemia signs plus frequent infections (↓WBC) plus bruising, nosebleeds, petechiae, gum bleeding (↓platelets). Headache | CBC shows pancytopenia with ↓retics. Bone marrow is mostly fat cells. ↑Hgb F | Transfusions, growth factors (filgrastim, epoetin), immunosuppressants. Stem cell transplant for severe disease (keep transfusions to a minimum to avoid sensitization). ⚠ Mortality >70% from hemorrhage and infection |
| **G6PD deficiency** | Missing enzyme causes RBCs to hemolyze early | Jaundice, dark urine (bilirubin), splenomegaly. Neonatal jaundice and kernicterus | ↑retics, **Heinz bodies** on the smear, DNA testing | Avoid triggering foods and drugs. Transfuse for severe hemolysis. Folic acid. ⚠ Kidney damage |
| **Polycythemia** | See next section | Pruritus (after a warm bath), facial flushing, headache, splenomegaly | ↑RBC, Hgb, Hct | Phlebotomy |

:::pearl Size clue
**Microcytic = iron deficiency (and lead). Macrocytic = B₁₂ or folate.** To tell B₁₂ from folate, check MMA: **↑MMA = B₁₂**, while **↑homocysteine with normal MMA = folate**. **Neuro symptoms mean B₁₂.**
:::

## Polycythemia vera (PV) · too many RBCs

:::matters Why it matters
- Rare: 5 per 1 million per year. More common in males, usually after age 60. **JAK2 gene mutation in 90%** (acquired after birth). Radiation exposure also raises risk.
- **Primary (PV):** a bone marrow disorder that overproduces **RBCs AND WBCs AND platelets**.
- **Secondary:** a response to hypoxia (high altitude, smoking, COPD, heart failure) through ↑erythropoietin, or an "inappropriate" response from kidney disease or EPO-secreting tumors. **WBCs and platelets stay NORMAL.** Treat the cause (e.g., stop smoking).
:::

:::patho Patho
Too many RBCs make the blood thick (**hyperviscosity**). Circulation slows and the heart labors, so even with more RBCs, tissue perfusion and O₂ delivery FALL (hypoxemia). The slow flow causes **clots**, and the abnormal platelets mean **bleeding** too.
**Lab definition:** Hct >48% (women) or >52% (men). Hgb >16.5 (women) or >18.5 (men) g/dL. RBC >6 million. WHO diagnosis: Hgb >16.5 (men) or >16.0 (women).
:::

:::ss Clinical manifestations ★
Slowly progressive, often found on routine labs. Caused by hyperviscosity, hypervolemia, and capillary engorgement.
- SOB, difficulty breathing lying flat
- Headache, dizziness, weakness, fatigue
- Blurred vision or blind spots
- **Splenomegaly** (about 75%), with fullness in the LUQ, early satiety, and weight loss
- **Generalized pruritus, especially after a WARM bath** (aquagenic)
- **Flushing of the face** (ruddy "plethora")
- Nosebleeds and bleeding gums. Angina and claudication.
:::

:::dx Diagnosis
- **CBC:** RBC >6 million, Hct can exceed 60%. ↑WBCs and platelets in PV (normal in secondary).
- **Bone marrow biopsy:** panmyelosis (overproduction of all 3 lines).
- **Genetic testing: JAK2.** **Erythropoietin level:** ↑ in secondary, **low in PV**.
- **Uric acid:** ↑ from rapid cell turnover, which can cause gout and kidney stones.
:::

:::tx Treatment: reduce viscosity, prevent hemorrhage
- **Therapeutic phlebotomy:** remove 350–500 mL weekly until Hct <45%, then as needed. Patients feel better right away as organ congestion drops.
- **Hydroxyurea or interferon** (or radiation) suppress the marrow when WBCs and platelets are high. Phlebotomy does not lower those.
- **Low-dose aspirin or anagrelide** reduce thrombosis.
- Medications for itching.
:::

:::watch Watch
- **THROMBOSIS (the main killer):** MI, stroke, PE, DVT. Sudden chest pain, headache, or SOB needs immediate attention.
- **Hemorrhage (GI)** from abnormal platelets.
- **Phlebotomy:** going too fast causes hypovolemia (↓BP, ↑HR, nausea), so check vital signs. Repeated phlebotomy causes IDA, so monitor ferritin.
- **Late:** myelofibrosis, transformation to AML, heart failure. Chemo and radiation carry a small risk of secondary cancer.
:::

:::nursing Nursing actions, and why
- **Dx:** ineffective tissue perfusion · activity intolerance · risk for ineffective coping
- Routine CBCs: keep **Hct <45% (men), <42% (women)** during phlebotomy, and monitor myelosuppression if on chemo.
- **Increase fluid intake** *→ why: dilutes the thick blood.*
- **Adequate rest** *→ why: the heart is working hard.*
- **Elevate legs when sitting** *→ why: helps venous return and prevents clots from stasis.*
- Modify CV risk factors: stop smoking, no crossed legs or restrictive clothing, DVT prophylaxis.
- **Teach:** signs of MI, stroke, and PE and to seek help immediately; why aggressive daily hydration matters; small frequent meals (abdominal fullness); avoid tight clothing and extreme temperatures; tepid baths ≤30 minutes, mild soap, pat dry, lotion, colloidal oatmeal; bleeding precautions; regular moderate exercise; regular labs; genetic testing.
:::

## Thrombocytopenia and bleeding disorders · platelets <150,000

:::matters Why it matters
- A complication or syndrome, not a disease by itself. It comes from ↓platelet production, ↑platelet use, or ↑destruction. Low platelets are the **most rapidly developing life-threatening cytopenia** because of the bleeding risk.
- **Risk factors:** malignancy, infection, meds (sulfa), autoimmune disease (SLE), DIC, African descent, obesity. ITP female:male is 2:1, peaking at ages 20–30. Gestational thrombocytopenia occurs in 6–15% of pregnancies (mild, resolves postpartum).
- **Acute ITP** (children 2–6): rapid onset, resolves in weeks to 6 months. **Chronic ITP** (women 15–40): insidious and rarely remits.
:::

:::ss Clinical manifestations (all types) ★
- **Easy bruising (ecchymosis) and PETECHIAE are the FIRST signs.** Petechiae do not blanch.
- Nosebleeds, bleeding gums, GI bleeding (black tarry stools), hematuria.
- **Wet purpura** (bruising of the buccal mucosa) is an early sign of worsening and usually needs treatment.
- Severe: bleeding into vital organs such as the brain can be fatal.
- **Thresholds:** start bleeding precautions <100,000, especially <50,000. Spontaneous bleeding <20,000, and platelets are usually transfused below 20,000 (or before procedures).
:::

### The 4 causes: tell them apart ★

| | Patho | Diagnosis | Treatment and ⚠ |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ITP** (immune / idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura) | **Antiplatelet antibodies (IgG)** cause the spleen to destroy platelets in hours instead of their normal 7–10 days. In children it follows a viral infection or immunization (70% have a virus 1–3 weeks before). In adults it can be drug-induced or autoimmune | CBC (<150,000). Usually a clinical diagnosis after ruling out other causes. Bone marrow biopsy if needed. Platelets >100,000: just observe | **Corticosteroids** (↓antibodies, ↓phagocytosis). **IVIG** (near-immediate rise). **Rituximab** (↓B cells). **Splenectomy** in extreme cases (the spleen is where platelets are destroyed) |
| **Hemophilia** | **X-linked**, so it shows up in males. A female carrier has a 50% chance of passing it to each son. **A = factor VIII, B = factor IX** (Christmas disease). Minor injuries bleed a lot. **Hemarthrosis** (bleeding into joints and muscles) causes deformity | Factor VIII and IX levels | **Replace the missing factor IV** (recombinant, no human blood) as soon as possible after injury: up to 3 daily doses for minor injuries, 7–10 days for major trauma or surgery |
| **DIC** | Secondary to severe trauma (crush, burns, sepsis, shock), OB complications (abruption, retained products), extensive surgery, cancers (leukemia, widespread mets), chemo. **Platelets and clotting factors are used up forming clots everywhere, then the patient bleeds from every orifice.** Microclots cause organ necrosis | **↑PT, PTT, and thrombin time, ↓fibrinogen, ↑fibrin split products and D-dimer** | **Treat the cause** (e.g., antibiotics for sepsis). Give blood, platelets, FFP, and **cryoprecipitate** (rich in factor VIII). ICU care. ⚠ High mortality |
| **HIT** (heparin-induced thrombocytopenia) | Immune system makes antibodies against heparin, usually **5–14 days** after it is started. Platelets are consumed making NEW CLOTS, so the patient usually **clots rather than bleeds**. Heparin flushes of central lines may contribute | Falling platelets while on heparin, with a new or enlarging thrombus | **STOP heparin first.** Give a different anticoagulant (**argatroban**, lepirudin). ⚠ **No warfarin** (causes skin gangrene). ⚠ **No platelet transfusion** (feeds more clots) |

:::nursing Nursing actions, and why
- **Dx:** risk of injury (bleeding) · fear · ineffective regimen management
- Assess platelet count, ecchymosis, petechiae and purpura, frank bleeding. In pregnancy, check LFTs and platelets and watch for postpartum hemorrhage.
- **Minimize blood loss:** direct pressure and/or ice for ≥5 minutes *→ why: vasoconstriction stops bleeding.*
- **Avoid IM injections** *→ why: bleeding into the muscle is hard to control.*
- **No rectal temps, enemas, suppositories, or douches** *→ why: they can tear thin mucosa.*
- **Safe, clutter-free room** *→ why: prevents bruising.*
- **Minimal BP cuff inflation**, only until the pulse disappears, and avoid automatic cuffs *→ why: overinflation bruises.*
- **Minimize blood draws** (consider a venous access device).
:::

:::nursing Box 34.1 Bleeding precautions ★
- Soft-bristle toothbrush or gauze. **No flossing.**
- **Electric razor only.**
- Shoes or slippers when out of bed (no bare feet). Clutter-free environment.
- **Daily stool softener** (no straining).
- No rectal thermometers, suppositories, enemas, or douches.
- No sexual intercourse when platelets are very low (slide: <50,000).
- **Do not blow your nose.**
- **Use a sharp knife** (a dull one needs more force).
- For a cut: direct pressure and/or ice ≥5 minutes.
- Keep IM injections, arterial sticks, and blood draws to a minimum.
- Avoid dental cleanings while platelets are low (case study). Know the emergency signs to report.
:::

## Leukemia · WBC malignancy

:::matters Why it matters
A malignant bone marrow disease in which leukocytes multiply uncontrollably. **Fatal if untreated.** Named by stem cell line and speed of onset:
- **AML and ALL** have an acute, sudden onset. **ALL is the most common childhood leukemia.**
- **CML and CLL** can be present for years but eventually turn acute.
- Most common in White males over 60.

**Risk factors:** genetic anomalies (**Philadelphia chromosome**, a 9;22 swap, in >90% of CML), **Down syndrome** (10–30× risk), **radiation or benzene** (the oil refinery in the case study), prior chemo or radiation, smoking.
:::

:::patho Patho
An unknown stimulus mutates a myeloid or lymphoid stem cell. It clones itself into immature **blasts** that never mature and don't undergo apoptosis, producing **leukocytosis**.
- Marrow, spleen, and lymph tissue become congested with blasts: lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, mucosal infiltration.
- The crowded marrow can't make enough RBCs and platelets, causing **ANEMIA and THROMBOCYTOPENIA**.
- Blasts can't fight infection, causing **neutropenia** and infections.
- Only cells that escape into the blood show up on a CBC, so early diagnosis is hard.
:::

:::ss Clinical manifestations ★
- Swollen lymph glands (neck, axillae, groin) and a swollen, painful LUQ (spleen)
- Increased infections and frequent low-grade fevers (from cytokines)
- **Anemia:** fatigue, pallor, weakness, SOB (worse on exertion or lying flat), dizziness
- **Bleeding:** bruising, petechiae, nosebleeds, bleeding gums
- **Bone pain** (marrow swelling in vertebrae, ribs, pelvis, long bones), gait changes
- Unintentional weight loss (high metabolic demand)
- It often presents as anemia or thrombocytopenia and may be found on a routine CBC in a patient with no symptoms. It can look like the flu.
:::

:::dx Diagnosis
- **CBC:** leukocytosis plus anemia plus thrombocytopenia.
- History and physical.
- **Flow cytometry** identifies the WBC markers and type. Can be run on blood if blasts are high; otherwise done on marrow.
- **Bone marrow aspirate and biopsy** confirm the type and extent.
- **Genetic testing** finds chromosome abnormalities that guide treatment.
:::

:::tx Treatment
- **Chemotherapy** (IV, intrathecal, oral, in combinations) in phases: **induction** (get remission) → **consolidation** (treat hidden disease) → **maintenance** (prevent relapse). **Remission ≠ cure.**
- **CNS involvement** is treated with radiation to the brain and spine *→ why: chemo can't cross the blood–brain barrier.*
- Supportive transfusions of RBCs and platelets.
- **Bone marrow / stem cell transplant** for refractory or relapsed disease. Donor cells come from a pelvic marrow harvest or peripheral pheresis after growth factor. ⚠ GVHD.
- **Immunotherapy:** monoclonal antibodies, and **CAR-T** (the patient's T cells are engineered to kill leukemia cells; the effect is permanent, and patients may live without B cells).
- **G-CSF growth factors (filgrastim) are generally not indicated in leukemia** *→ why: they may fuel the disease, since it comes from hematopoietic stem cells* (although the slide case did give filgrastim).
:::

:::watch Complications (mostly from treatment)
- **MYELOSUPPRESSION** from chemo or radiation leads to neutropenia, which brings a high risk of life-threatening infection. **Neutropenic precautions when ANC ≤500.**
- **Fever in a patient on chemo means broad-spectrum antibiotics IMMEDIATELY.** Fever is often the first and only sign of infection, and infection is the most common cause of death besides the disease itself.
- **Hemorrhage** from thrombocytopenia. Report bruising, petechiae, nosebleeds, conjunctival hemorrhage, bleeding gums, hemoptysis, hematuria, or GI bleeding right away.
- Anemia needs frequent RBC transfusions.
- Long-term organ damage: renal failure, cardiomyopathy, pulmonary fibrosis, neurocognitive and liver damage. Sterility.
:::

:::nursing Nursing
- **Dx:** risk for infection · risk for impaired tissue perfusion · risk for bleeding · risk for compromised family coping
- **Assess:** vital signs (fever), anemia signs, bruising and petechiae (non-blanching), CBC and WBC.
- **Actions:** give chemo as prescribed. Neutropenic precautions. Prophylactic antibiotics, antivirals, and antifungals. **IV antibiotics immediately if ANC <500 with infection signs.** Symptom management (antiemetics, antidiarrheals, mouth sores). Blood products.
- **Teach:** neutropenic and bleeding precautions, signs of anemia and thrombocytopenia, the disease course, chemo and radiation reactions, **sterility risk (bank sperm or eggs before therapy)**.
- **Outcomes:** remission, fewer complications, less anemia, infection, and bleeding.
:::

:::nursing Box 34.2 Neutropenic precautions ★
- Frequent hand washing or alcohol sanitizer, for everyone.
- Avoid crowds. Wear a mask if going out. Avoid sick people, small children, and pets.
- Wash raw fruits and vegetables well. **Avoid raspberries and blackberries** (bumpy and hard to wash).
- Check temperature daily and **call for >100.4°F (38°C)**. For fever, rigors, or illness, seek care immediately (IV antibiotics prevent sepsis).
- **No live plants or cut flowers** (bacteria, mold), at home or in the hospital.
- No standing water (humidifiers).
- Take prophylactic antimicrobials daily and finish the whole course.
- Private room if hospitalized.
- **No rectal temps, suppositories, or enemas** *→ why: rectal bacteria can enter the blood.*
:::

## Malignant lymphomas · Hodgkin's vs non-Hodgkin's

:::matters Why it matters
- **Hodgkin's lymphoma** is about 14% of lymphomas. Defined by **Reed–Sternberg cells** (giant lymphocytes) and has a **better survival rate**. Peaks in young adulthood and again after 55. Risks: Epstein–Barr virus, a first-degree relative with it.
- **Non-Hodgkin's lymphoma** has dozens of B- and T-cell subtypes and causes about 4% of cancers and 4% of cancer deaths (1 in 50 lifetime). More common in males. Risks: pesticides (farming), autoimmune disease, organ transplant (immunosuppressants), HIV, EBV, HHV-8, H. pylori, prior chemo or radiation.
:::

:::patho Patho and staging
A single progenitor cell in a lymph node becomes a solid tumor that evades apoptosis. It collects where lymphocytes gather (nodes, mediastinum), spreads through lymph tissue and the spleen, and then reaches other organs. Spread beyond nodes is called **extranodal**.
**Staging (WHO):** I = one node · II = ≥2 nodes on the same side of the diaphragm · III = both sides · IV = organs plus nodes. "E" = extranodal, "S" = spleen, plus bulky disease.
:::

:::ss Clinical manifestations ★
- **PAINLESS swelling of lymph nodes** (cervical, axillary, inguinal, breast)
- **"B symptoms":** low-grade fevers with no clear cause, **DRENCHING NIGHT SWEATS**, unintentional weight loss. These signal a heavier disease burden.
- Fatigue, anemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia
- **Generalized pruritus with excoriation, common in Hodgkin's**
- **Dry, persistent cough that is worse lying flat** from a mediastinal mass, which can compress the trachea (airway risk)
- SOB and fullness when the spleen or liver is involved, or the vena cava or spine is compressed
:::

:::dx Diagnosis
- History and physical. CBC (marrow involvement). **Uric acid and LDH are ↑** from high cell turnover. Viral testing rules out infection.
- Imaging: chest x-ray, CT, PET for extent and staging.
- **Lymph node biopsy is definitive.**
- Bone marrow biopsy if marrow involvement is suspected. Lumbar puncture if CNS involvement is suspected.
:::

:::tx Treatment
- **Chemotherapy is the primary treatment**, plus radiation and/or immunotherapy. Lymphoma is sensitive and responds quickly.
- Surgery to remove the tumor is not typically done. Splenectomy is now rare.
- Indolent (slow) lymphoma may get a wait-and-see approach.
- Stem cell transplant for relapse or poor response. Autologous if the marrow is not involved ("rescue" after high-dose therapy).
:::

:::watch Complications
- **Superior vena cava syndrome ⚠ EMERGENCY:** edema of the face and arms, SOB, chest pain, dysphagia.
- **Spinal cord compression ⚠ EMERGENCY:** neuro deficits, paralysis.
- **Hypercalcemia (>11 mg/dL) ⚠ EMERGENCY:** from bone mets, causing dehydration, polyuria, confusion, heart block.
- Myelodysplasia, hepatic or renal failure, hyperviscosity, venous thrombosis (leg pain or swelling).
:::

:::nursing Nursing
- **Dx:** risk for infection · activity intolerance · risk for ineffective coping
- **Assess:** vital signs and B symptoms · pain, breathing, face and neck (SVC) · neuro status (cord compression) · nodes · skin (excoriation from itching) · CBC · lower extremities (clot) · serum calcium · activity tolerance.
- **Actions:** blood products, chemo, IV fluids *→ why: replace losses from fevers and sweats and dilute calcium*, ease itching and night sweats, routine CBCs, emotional support.
- **Teach:** disease, infection risk, keep the treatment schedule, rest often, nutrition, report SOB, facial or arm swelling, chest pain, fever, or neuro changes, infertility and fertility preservation.
- **Outcomes:** quality of life, no bleeding, no infection.
:::

## Multiple myeloma · plasma cell cancer, incurable

:::matters Why it matters
13% of hematologic cancers, about 5–7 per 100,000 per year. **Incurable** B-cell malignancy. Average age 62. 2× more common in people of African descent, and more common in males. Mean survival about 3 years (46.6% alive at 5 years).
**Risk factors:** herbicides, insecticides, petroleum, heavy metals, plastics, radiation. **Precursors:** MGUS (monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance) and solitary plasmacytoma.
:::

:::patho Patho
One clone of plasma cells (mature B cells that make antibodies) overgrows the marrow and pumps out monoclonal immunoglobulin (IgG or IgA), called the **"M protein"** (a monoclonal gammopathy).
- An osteoclast and osteoblast imbalance causes **lytic bone lesions**, which release calcium (hypercalcemia).
- Excess light chains (**Bence Jones protein** in urine) are directly toxic to the kidney.
:::

:::ss Clinical manifestations ★
- **BONE PAIN, especially the BACK (and hips)**, from lytic destruction
- Fatigue, weakness, pallor (normocytic anemia)
- Recurrent infections
- Weight loss
- Paresthesia, numbness and tingling (nerve root compression)
- Hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, renal stones
- **6 cardinal features:** lytic bone disease · M protein · anemia · renal insufficiency · hypercalcemia · infection tendency
:::

:::dx Diagnosis: CRAB ★
- **C** hyperCalcemia (>11.5 mg/dL)
- **R** Renal insufficiency (creatinine >2 mg/dL)
- **A** Anemia (Hgb <10 g/dL)
- **B** Bone disease (lytic lesions, osteopenia, pathologic fractures)
- Plasma cells ≥10% in marrow, M protein in serum or urine.
- Serum and urine protein electrophoresis, free light chains, Bence Jones protein in urine, β2-microglobulin (tumor burden), CBC, Ca, creatinine, uric acid.
- Bone marrow aspiration, skeletal survey, bone density. **MRI if there is back pain** (rule out cord compression).
:::

:::tx Treatment
- **Bisphosphonates** *→ why: reduce bone breakdown, so fewer fractures and lower calcium.*
- Radiation
- **DVT prophylaxis** (thrombus prophylaxis)
- **Dexamethasone** (corticosteroid)
- High-dose chemo: **thalidomide, lenalidomide, bortezomib.** ⚠ Thalidomide and lenalidomide are **teratogenic**.
- Autologous stem cell transplant
:::

:::watch Complications
- **Pathologic fractures.** Sudden severe pain, or pain in a new spot, especially the back, may mean a fracture or vertebral collapse.
- Spinal cord compression causing numbness, tingling, and motor changes.
- Hypercalcemia. Renal failure (Bence Jones protein, stones). Anemia, marrow failure, thrombocytopenia and bleeding, infection. Chemo side effects.
- Bisphosphonates and denosumab can cause **osteonecrosis of the jaw** with long-term use (Ch 13).
:::

:::nursing Nursing
- **Dx:** acute or chronic pain · impaired mobility · risk for infection · risk for injury (fractures) · impaired urinary output · fear
- **Assess:** vital signs, fatigue, pain, paresthesias, I&O, serum and urine calcium, BUN and creatinine *→ why: kidney damage from light chains and calcium.*
- **Actions:** chemo, bisphosphonates, pain meds. **Encourage fluids** *→ why: dilute calcium and protect the kidneys.* Position for comfort (eases nerve compression). **Encourage ambulation** *→ why: weight-bearing builds bone and prevents DVT and deconditioning.*
- **Teach:** report fatigue, SOB, dizziness, or confusion · sudden severe pain · changes in sensation or motor function · nonpharmacologic pain management.
- **Outcomes:** pain controlled, optimal renal function, no infection.
:::

## Leukemia vs lymphoma vs myeloma ★

| | Leukemia | Lymphoma | Multiple myeloma |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cell / site** | WBC stem cell in the bone marrow (blasts) | Lymphocyte in a lymph node (solid tumor) | Plasma cell (mature B) in the marrow, making M protein |
| **Hallmark sign** | Anemia, bleeding, and infection from a crowded marrow. Bone pain | Painless nodes plus B symptoms (fever, drenching night sweats, weight loss), itching (Hodgkin's) | Back and bone pain, CRAB, Bence Jones protein |
| **Key risk** | Benzene and radiation, Down syndrome, prior chemo, smoking | EBV (Hodgkin's), pesticides, HIV, transplant (NHL) | Herbicides, petroleum, heavy metals, MGUS, African descent |
| **Definitive dx** | Flow cytometry, bone marrow biopsy | Lymph node biopsy | Marrow plasma cells ≥10%, M protein, CRAB |
| **Main treatment** | Chemo (induction → consolidation → maintenance), transplant, CAR-T | Chemo ± radiation and immunotherapy (surgery not typical) | Bisphosphonates, dexamethasone, chemo, autologous transplant, DVT prophylaxis |
| **Big danger** | Infection (neutropenia), hemorrhage | SVC syndrome, cord compression, hypercalcemia | Fractures, renal failure, hypercalcemia, cord compression |

## Case: Mr. Owens, 62, AML
He has worked in an oil refinery since his teens (**benzene, his highest risk factor**) and smoked for 40+ years. Fatigue, SOB on stairs, bleeding gums. CBC: RBC 3.2, Hgb 9.4, Hct 28.1, WBC 6.2, platelets 97,000. A bone marrow biopsy confirms AML, treated with **daunorubicin + cytarabine (Ara-C)**. Two weeks later: Hgb 7.9, **ANC 0.6** (severe neutropenia), platelets 19,000. He gets PRBCs, platelets, filgrastim, and prophylactic antibiotics, antivirals, and antifungals. Remission follows.
- **Priority after chemo: INFECTION** (ANC 0.6).
- **Go to the ED for:** temperature 102°F, chills, a nosebleed lasting more than 10 minutes.
- **Teaching gap:** "I have an appointment to get my teeth cleaned."
- *SLIDE vs BOOK:* platelets were 19,000 on the slide and 12,000 in the book.

## Class notes add-on (NUR 291)

:::note Iron deficiency anemia
- Most prevalent nutritional deficiency in the world; risks include poor diet, hemorrhage, chronic blood loss, GI problems, lead paint.
- **Ferritin less than 100 indicates IDA (slide).**
- Know the diet: red meat, green leafy vegetables, vitamin C, oranges, grapefruit.
- **Iron is taken with vitamin C on an empty stomach for best absorption.** *(class only)*
:::

:::note Polycythemia vera (all purple in your notes)
- **Hyperviscosity:** the blood is thick, increased production of RBCs.
- Risk for blood clot and stroke.
- Presents with SOB, **orthopnea** (difficulty breathing lying flat), swollen spleen / splenomegaly, headache, dizziness, blurred vision.
- Diagnosis: labs, bone marrow testing, genetic testing.
- Treatment from class: reduce hyperviscosity, prevent hemorrhage, **aggressive IV fluid hydration**. *(class only: IV)*
:::

:::note Thrombocytopenia
- **Number 1 side effect is bleeding.**
- **Most common medication cause: Bactrim** *(class only; the guide says "sulfa," and Bactrim is a sulfa drug).*
- Higher risk for women.
:::

:::note Leukemia and lymphoma
- Leukemia: teach neutropenic precautions (Box 34.2).
- Lymphoma manifestations: painless swelling of lymph nodes, low-grade fever.
- Lymphoma complication: superior vena cava syndrome.
:::

:::note Multiple myeloma and pain
- **Think calcium** (hypercalcemia, CRAB).
- Medical management: bisphosphonates, e.g. **Boniva (ibandronate)** *(class only: Boniva by name).*
- Nursing: **encourage fluids**, because the extra calcium can cause stones.
- Pain: know your pain medications. **Know slide 22:** why we use opioids vs nonopioids. Nonopioids (acetaminophen, NSAIDs) for mild pain and anti-inflammation; opioids for moderate to severe pain; in cancer, use the WHO ladder and dose around the clock.
:::
