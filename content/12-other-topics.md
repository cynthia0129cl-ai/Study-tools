## Hospice vs. palliative care · 2 questions

| | Palliative care | Hospice care |
|---|---|---|
| **When** | **Any stage**, from diagnosis of a serious illness (Ch 26: starts on day 1) | **End of life:** prognosis of **≤6 months** if the disease runs its usual course |
| **Curative treatment?** | **Yes**, can continue alongside chemo, radiation, surgery | **No:** patient stops curative treatment and chooses comfort care |
| **Goal** | Relieve symptoms (pain, dyspnea, N/V), improve quality of life for patient and family | Comfort, dignity, peaceful death; family support including bereavement after death |
| **Who qualifies** | Anyone with serious illness, any age | Terminal illness certified by physicians (Medicare hospice benefit) |
| **Where** | Hospital, clinic, home | Usually home; also hospice facility, nursing home |

:::pearl Remember
**All hospice is palliative, but not all palliative care is hospice.** "Palliative care is not the same as hospice" (Ch 26). Patients can leave hospice to restart curative treatment.
:::

:::nursing Signs death is near, and nursing care
- **Signs:** ↓ intake and urine output · mottled, cool extremities · Cheyne-Stokes breathing / apnea periods · noisy secretions ("death rattle") · ↓ LOC · **hearing is often the last sense lost.**
- **Nursing:** manage pain (around the clock; opioids/morphine for dyspnea), mouth care, repositioning, emotional and spiritual support, honor advance directives/DNR.
:::

## Organ donation: keep organs viable
- **Ventilator:** transplant needs viable organs, so after brain death the patient is kept on the ventilator (and hemodynamic support) to keep oxygen and blood flowing to the organs until recovery.
- **Brain death** = irreversible loss of all brain function (including brainstem), declared by a physician. The heart can keep beating with ventilator support.
- **Maintain organ perfusion:** BP/MAP support (fluids, vasopressors), oxygenation, normal temperature, urine output, electrolytes (**diabetes insipidus** is common).
- Hospitals must notify the **organ procurement organization (OPO)** of every death or imminent death; trained OPO staff usually approach the family.
- **Tissues** (corneas, skin, bone, heart valves) can be donated after cardiac death; most solid organs need a perfused donor.
- Family pays no donation costs; open-casket funeral is still possible; most major religions support donation.

## Dosage calculation · 2 questions

| Type | Formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Basic (desired over have)** | (D ÷ H) × Q | Order 500 mg; have 250 mg/5 mL → (500 ÷ 250) × 5 = **10 mL** |
| **Weight-based** | mg/kg × kg (lb ÷ 2.2 = kg) | 15 mg/kg for 44 lb → 20 kg × 15 = **300 mg** |
| **IV pump rate** | mL ÷ hr = mL/hr | 1,000 mL over 8 h = **125 mL/hr** |
| **Gravity drip** | (mL × drop factor) ÷ minutes = gtt/min | 1,000 mL over 8 h, 15 gtt/mL → (1,000 × 15) ÷ 480 = **31 gtt/min** |
| **Infusion time** | Total mL ÷ mL/hr = hours | 500 mL at 100 mL/hr = **5 h** |
| **Concentration (heparin, units)** | Ordered units/hr ÷ units/mL = mL/hr | 1,000 units/hr; 25,000 units/250 mL (100 units/mL) → **10 mL/hr** |

**Conversions:** 1 kg = 2.2 lb · 1 g = 1,000 mg · 1 mg = 1,000 mcg · 1 tsp = 5 mL · 1 tbsp = 15 mL · 1 oz = 30 mL. Round per your program's rules (usually tenths for mL, whole numbers for gtt). **Check units before you calculate.**
