## Bioterrorism PowerPoint (your uploaded slides)

**Definition:** terrorism involving the intentional release or dissemination of biological agents (bacteria, viruses, insects, fungi, or toxins), natural or human-modified. An attack may not be obvious: usually local health care workers report a **pattern of unusual illness** or a wave of sick people seeking emergency care. The first evidence may be symptoms of the disease. Officials may not immediately know what the illness is, how to treat it, or who is in danger.

### CDC categories (A, B, C)
:::slides Category A · highest priority
Pathogens that pose a risk to national security because they (1) can be easily disseminated or transmitted person to person, (2) cause high mortality and major public health impact, (3) might cause public panic and social disruption, and (4) require special action for public health preparedness.

**Agents:** Anthrax (*Bacillus anthracis*) · Botulism (*Clostridium botulinum* toxin) · Plague (*Yersinia pestis*) · Smallpox (variola major) · Tularemia (*Francisella tularensis*) · Viral hemorrhagic fevers, including filoviruses (Ebola, Marburg) and arenaviruses (Lassa, Machupo).
:::

:::slides Category B · second highest priority
Moderately easy to disseminate; moderate morbidity and low mortality; require specific enhancements of CDC's diagnostic capacity and enhanced disease surveillance.

**Agents:** Brucellosis · Epsilon toxin of *Clostridium perfringens* · Food safety threats (*Salmonella*, *E. coli* O157:H7, *Shigella*) · Glanders (*Burkholderia mallei*) · Melioidosis (*Burkholderia pseudomallei*) · Psittacosis (*Chlamydia psittaci*) · Q fever (*Coxiella burnetii*) · Ricin toxin (castor beans) · Staphylococcal enterotoxin B · Typhus fever (*Rickettsia prowazekii*) · Viral encephalitis (alphaviruses: eastern, Venezuelan, western equine encephalitis) · Water safety threats (*Vibrio cholerae*, *Cryptosporidium parvum*).
:::

:::slides Category C · third priority
Emerging pathogens that could be engineered for mass dissemination in the future because of availability, ease of production and dissemination, and potential for high morbidity and mortality.

**Agents:** Influenza (novel strain) · Nipah virus · Typhus disease (*Rickettsia prowazekii*) · Viral encephalitis (alphaviruses) · Water safety threats (*Vibrio cholerae*, *Cryptosporidium parvum*).
:::

### Anthrax (Category A)
- Sporulating gram-positive rod. Human disease mostly through **contact with animal products** (skins). In **2001, 22 cases** occurred in the US through the postal system.
- Spores enter subcutaneously or by inhalation, become the vegetative form, and replicate. A thick capsule avoids phagocytosis; edema factor and lethal factor toxins drive edema, hemorrhage, and necrosis.
- **Inhalational:** incubation 1–7 days; within 24 hours respiratory failure, **hemorrhagic mediastinitis**, necrotizing pneumonia, shock, multiorgan failure, death. Shock plus hemorrhagic mediastinitis is the clinical hallmark.
- **Cutaneous:** incubation 7–14 days. Painless, possibly pruritic papule → central vesicle → **painless black eschar.** Edema, regional lymphadenopathy, fever, malaise. Hands, arms, face, neck.
- **GI:** from undercooked meat of infected animals; bowel edema, mesenteric lymphadenitis and necrosis, rapid shock and death.
- **Diagnosis:** culture of blood, sputum, pleural fluid, CSF, or skin. **Treatment: ciprofloxacin, doxycycline.**

### Smallpox (variola, Category A)
- Poxviridae. **Eradicated 1977**, now a bioterrorism concern: unvaccinated population, contagious, spreads by **aerosol.** Only two stockpiles remain (CDC and Russian State Research Center).
- **Five clinical categories:** ordinary (macule → papule → vesicle → pustule) · modified (more rapid but less severe) · flat · **hemorrhagic (more common in pregnant females)** · variola sine eruptione.
- **Diagnosis primarily clinical;** RT-PCR only at the CDC. **Treatment supportive;** some evidence cidofovir has activity.

### Plague (Category A)
- *Yersinia pestis*. Transmitted by rodent-infected flea bites, animal scratches/bites, exposure to infected humans, bioterrorism.
- **Bubonic:** sudden fever, chills, headache, then painful swollen regional lymph node (**bubo**, intensely tender, erythema, edema; fluctuation test negative). **Septicemic:** sepsis without a bubo. **Pneumonic:** short incubation, rapid **ARDS.**
- Diagnosis: culture of sputum or blood; ELISA/PCR mainly field testing. **Treatment: streptomycin** (drug of choice); gentamicin or doxycycline.

### Tularemia (Category A)
- *Francisella tularensis*, gram-negative, zoonotic. Spread by ticks and flies, infected animals, improperly prepared meat, animal bites, contaminated water, aerosol. **No human-to-human transmission.**
- Patterns: ulceroglandular, pneumonic, glandular, oropharyngeal, oculoglandular. Hard to culture; diagnosis by ELISA or histology. X-ray: patchy bilateral opacities, hilar adenopathy.
- **Treatment: gentamicin** (drug of choice); doxycycline and ciprofloxacin second choice.

### Category B agents
- **Glanders** (*Burkholderia mallei*): disease of **horses**; humans infected by contact or droplet. Acute or chronic skin infection; droplets cause pneumonia and sepsis rapidly. Often misclassified on culture. **Imipenem** (3rd-gen cephalosporin, ciprofloxacin alternatives).
- **Melioidosis** (*B. pseudomallei*): soil and water reservoir; contact or inhalation; aerosol → pneumonia; sepsis and MODS rapidly. Imipenem or 3rd-gen cephalosporin, then 20 days of doxycycline or cotrimoxazole.
- **Psittacosis** (*Chlamydophila psittaci*): birds (parrots, cockatiels, canaries). Aerosol → "atypical pneumonia" (cough, fever, headache). ELISA. **Macrolide, doxycycline, or ciprofloxacin.**
- **Q fever** (*Coxiella burnetii*): cattle, sheep, goats (bacteria in milk, urine, feces, amniotic fluid, placenta); droplet spread. **Fever >104°F**, severe headache, myalgia, sore throat, nonproductive cough, N/V/D, chest pain; lasts 1–2 weeks; 30–50% develop pneumonia; endocarditis and chronic Q fever (>6 months) rare. **Doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 15–21 days.**

### Role of the public health system
- **Being prepared:** educate medical community and public · train special response teams · exercises for different scenarios · communication systems · interagency protocols.
- **Initial response:** early detection through surveillance/rapid assessment · mobilize laboratory · rapid confirmation of agent, site, at-risk population, prophylaxis/treatment · alert medical community, ERs, labs · implement disease-specific plans (e.g., smallpox) · determine resource needs and possible quarantine · coordinate with partner agencies.
- **Continued response:** monitor communication network · provide accurate, timely information to the public · continue epidemiologic investigation · assess environmental contamination · provide testing/prophylaxis/treatment to at-risk population · access biological stockpiles as necessary.
