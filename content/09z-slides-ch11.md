## Chapter 11 PowerPoint, word for word

:::slides Slide 1 · Why pain matters
Most universal symptom · Significant public health problem 1 of 3 ED chief complaints is pain · 60% of patients report pain at primary care visits Costs $100 billion per year
:::

:::slides Slide 2 · Gate control theory
Two brain–spinal cord diagrams. Left: high-intensity pain goes up the ascending pathway, gate is open.

Right: emotion, motivation, memory, and cognition send signals down the inhibitory descending pathway, gate is closed.
:::

:::slides Slide 3 · Classifying pain by duration
Acute pain – short duration, hours to weeks, sudden onset, alerts and prompts withdrawal, avoidance, predictable in course; intensity improves Chronic pain – long term, months–years, sustained or spontaneous, disrupts normal activities, unpredictable in course intensity, inconsistently responsive to both common and adjuvant pain management strategies
:::

:::slides Slide 4 · Classifying pain by origin
Nociceptive pain: cutaneous, somatic, visceral · Neuropathic pain
:::

:::slides Slide 5 · How pain signals travel and become chronic
Injury in the hand → peripheral nociceptor → ascending pathway in the spinal cord → brain perceives pain → descending pathway back down.
:::

:::slides Slide 6 · Why two people feel the same injury differently
Prior experience with pain · Expectations of pain · Anxiety Sociocultural determinants: values and expectations, access to healthcare resources, language barriers Gender: women have lower pain thresholds, rate noxious stimuli as more painful, have lower tolerance for intense pain; differences present after puberty; men and women respond differently to analgesia Genetics: pain threshold, pain tolerance, response to analgesics Pain and older adults: mixed evidence on prevalence and pattern of pain; decrease in acute pain
:::

:::slides Slide 7 · The 0–10 scale and its cousins
0–10 scale · Measure of pain intensity Numeric rating scales · Verbal rating scales · Visual analog scales

Figure: Simple Descriptive Scale, 0–10 Numeric Scale, Visual Analog Scale (VAS), and a pain thermometer
:::

:::slides Slide 8 · OPQRST-AAA + physical exam
OPQRST-AAA: Onset · Quality · Region and radiation · Severity · Time and duration · Aggravating/alleviating factors and associated clinical manifestations Physical exam: range of motion, neurological assessment, motor function Multidisciplinary team: physical therapist, pharmacist, social worker
:::

:::slides Slide 9 · Partnership, care planning & nursing diagnoses
Patient and nurse – the therapeutic partnership: assessment of pain, assessment of functional status, interviewing Care-planning process: data collection, evaluation of data, form diagnostic hypotheses Nursing diagnoses (full list below)

Measuring effectiveness of care: review of laboratory values, structured questioning, length and quality of sleep
:::

:::slides Slide 10 · NSAIDs, aspirin & acetaminophen
Pharmacological options – nonopioid analgesics: NSAIDs Monitoring risks and benefits: prostaglandin synthesis – chemicals released that stimulate inflammation; COX-1 & COX-2 COX-1 – harmful to GI, disrupts protective lining · COX-2 – both anti-inflammatory & antipyretic Inhibit platelet aggregation · Thrombocytopenia – reduced platelet count
:::

:::slides Slide 11 · Anti-inflammatory steroids
Pharmacological options – corticosteroids: anti-inflammatory
:::

:::slides Slide 12 · Numb the area, stay awake
Local anesthetics: topically applied; injected into nerves and tissue
:::

:::slides Slide 13 · Counterirritants and capsaicin
Topical rubefacients: topically applied menthol-containing ointments; cause local vasodilation; produce cool sensation
:::

:::slides Slide 14 · Receptors, potency & side effects
Opioids: bind to opioid receptors in the brain; available via many routes

Figure (continued_6): relative potency chart of opioids and NSAIDs compared with morphine, from most potent (top) to least potent (bottom)
:::

:::slides Slide 15 · Adjuvants for tough pain
Clonidine · Tricyclic antidepressants – Elavil · Glutamate receptor antagonist (principal excitatory neurotransmitter in CNS) – Ketalar · Ion-channel blockers – lidocaine
:::

:::slides Slide 16 · Choosing the right drug and route
Decision making for effective dose, route, and medication choice: best pain relief, fewest adverse effects
:::

:::slides Slide 17 · WHO principles & three-step ladder
WHO principles: analgesics given at regular intervals; administered according to pain intensity; goal is to improve functional quality of life; individualized dosing; attention to dose and response; evaluation of therapeutic response WHO three-step ladder (figure)
:::

:::slides Slide 18 · Positioning, heat/cold, rom, massage
Positioning · Heat and cold · Range of motion and exercise
:::

:::slides Slide 19 · Refocusing attention
Music therapy · Guided imagery · Hypnosis · Prayer
:::

:::slides Slide 20 · TENS, DBS & expectancy
Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation · Deep brain stimulation – lead is placed subcutaneously; also tx for PD tremors
:::

:::slides Slide 21 · Older adults & cognitive impairment
Older adults: underdiagnosed, undertreated, ineffective management → depression, anxiety, impaired mobility, social isolation, falls, sleep disturbance, reduced food and fluid intake, malnutrition Adults with cognitive or communication impairments: self-report, tests of cognition, observations of behavior, changes in routine behavior
:::
